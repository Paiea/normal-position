# Keoni's Tools

The workshop looked worse in daylight.

Malia had been inside it plenty of times.

Enough to know where the door was.

Enough to know not to lean against anything without checking first.

Enough to know that Keoni could reach into what looked like a pile of unrelated metal and come back with the exact washer he wanted.

That was not the same as understanding the room.

She discovered this at nine in the morning when she tried to help.

Keoni was standing at the main bench with Kimo's winch open in front of him.

The replacement ratchet sat beside it.

A second ratchet sat farther back.

Three springs.

Two pawls.

Four bolts.

A rag.

A shallow metal tray full of parts Malia could not identify.

On the floor beneath the bench were two plastic bins.

One said GOOD.

One said MAYBE.

Malia looked at them.

"That's your system?"

Keoni did not look up.

"Part of it."

She crouched.

GOOD contained rusted metal.

MAYBE contained cleaner metal.

Malia stared.

"You labeled these backward."

"No."

"That one has rust."

"Surface."

"This one looks new."

"Wrong shaft."

She looked at him.

"Then why keep it?"

"Could fit something else."

"Then why is it in maybe?"

"Because maybe."

Malia stood.

"I hate this room."

"Room doesn't care."

She almost answered.

Then stopped.

Too easy.

Keoni noticed.

One corner of his mouth moved.

Malia pointed at the main bench.

"What can I actually do?"

"Nothing."

"You asked me to come in here."

"I asked if you wanted coffee."

"You said it from the workshop."

"Still counts."

She looked around.

The room smelled like oil, wet rope, cut metal, and the faint sweet chemical smell of something she did not want identified.

Tools occupied the walls, but not cleanly.

Some hung from pegboard.

Some lay flat in shallow drawers.

Some sat in jars.

Three wrenches lived on a nail by themselves.

A hammer with a cracked red handle rested under the window.

Two screwdrivers were stuck into a coffee can with drill bits, markers, and what appeared to be a toothbrush.

There were shelves of parts in clear containers.

Those looked promising.

Malia walked over.

Labels.

Finally.

FUEL

ELEC

PUMP

SS HARDWARE

BRASS

UNKNOWN

She pointed.

"Unknown."

Keoni said, "Useful category."

"That's not a category."

"It is until I know."

"What happens when you know?"

"Move it."

Malia opened the UNKNOWN bin.

Keoni looked up.

"Don't."

She closed it.

"I was looking."

"Look through plastic."

"You have trust issues."

"You rearrange cups for recreation."

"Fair."

She stepped back.

On the lower shelf were three starter motors.

One had blue tape around the housing.

One had a white paint mark.

One had nothing.

"Those all work?"

"One."

"Which?"

Keoni pointed without looking.

"Blue."

"What are the other two doing here?"

"One can be rebuilt. One has good parts."

"Which is which?"

"White rebuild. Plain parts."

Malia stared.

"You remember all that?"

"Yeah."

"Why not write it?"

"Blue tape."

"That tells you one thing."

"It tells me enough."

"Until you forget."

"Then I test it."

That annoyed her more than it should have.

At the hotel, systems were built so the next person did not need the first person's memory.

Labels.

Checklists.

Par levels.

Standard locations.

A room that depended entirely on one worker's head was a bad room.

She said so.

Keoni kept working.

"At your hotel."

"Anywhere."

"No."

"Yes."

"No."

"Why?"

He set the ratchet down.

Then he looked at her.

"Because this isn't inventory."

"It is literally inventory."

"No. It's work waiting."

Malia folded her arms.

"Explain."

Keoni pointed at the bench.

"Kimo's winch is current."

Then the blue-taped starter.

"That belongs to Haku's outboard. Paid deposit. Waiting for gasket."

White-marked starter.

"Mine. Rebuild when I have time."

Plain starter.

"Parts."

He pointed at the three wrenches on the separate nail.

"Those go with the press."

"Why not with the other wrenches?"

"Because I use them with the press."

"The toothbrush?"

"Carburetors."

"Coffee can?"

"Stuff I grab standing here."

"Cracked hammer?"

"Balanced right."

"That's not a thing."

"It is if you use it."

Malia looked at the hammer.

"Why not replace the handle?"

"I will when it gets worse."

"That seems like how you end up with half a boat on a trailer."

Keoni looked at her.

"Different kind of worse."

"Convenient."

"Materially different."

She hated that he was probably right.

Keoni went back to the winch.

Malia moved around the room more carefully.

Once she stopped asking where objects belonged in general, the workshop began separating into smaller territories.

Current jobs near the main bench.

Electrical work near the west wall.

Fuel parts on the shelf by the window.

Boat hardware lower because it was heavier.

Rope and line outside the inner work zone.

Scrap close to the side door because eventually it left.

Eventually.

She found a pile of aluminum brackets on a shelf.

"Trash?"

"No."

"All bent."

"That's why they're useful."

Malia stared.

Keoni sighed.

"For templates."

"Oh."

"Broken tells you shape sometimes."

That stayed with her.

She picked up nothing.

Progress.

Koa came through the door carrying the poster board from yesterday.

Not the whole project.

One detached panel.

The corner had come loose from a thin plastic frame.

"Dad."

Keoni looked.

"No."

"You didn't even see."

"I see you."

Koa held up the panel.

"It broke."

"Use tape."

"Looks ugly."

Malia said, "Correct."

Koa pointed at her.

"See?"

Keoni looked at the frame.

"Need two zip ties."

"Where?"

Keoni pointed at a drawer.

Koa opened the wrong one.

"Other."

He opened another.

Pulled out a bag.

"Cutters?"

Keoni pointed toward the pegboard.

Koa reached.

Empty hook.

He looked around.

"Dad."

Keoni looked at the empty hook.

Then at the bench.

Then at Koa.

"You had them."

"No."

"Yesterday."

"No."

"Poster."

"We used scissors."

One of Koa's friends appeared behind him.

"The black scissors."

Koa looked back.

"Yeah."

The girl said, "Then you used the cutter thing because the scissors sucked."

Koa stared at her.

"Traitor."

She shrugged.

Keoni held out his hand.

"Where?"

Koa thought.

"Lanai?"

"Go."

Koa left.

Malia looked at the empty hook.

"Side cutters?"

"Yeah."

"You knew he had them because the hook was empty."

"No."

"What else?"

Keoni pointed to a small white shaving on the workbench.

Malia leaned closer.

Plastic.

A clipped tail from a zip tie.

"That could be anything."

"It's from mine."

"Your zip ties have DNA?"

"Width."

He picked up the clipping.

"These are the small ones I keep here. Koa used some yesterday for the poster frame."

Malia looked toward the lanai.

"You saw him?"

"No."

"So you inferred."

"Yeah."

She smiled.

"Welcome to my life."

Keoni shook his head.

"No."

"That is exactly what I do."

"No."

"Object moved. Residue. User."

"You're missing the important part."

"Which is?"

"I know what the tool does."

Malia stopped.

Keoni held up the white clipping.

"And who uses it badly."

From outside Koa shouted, "Found it."

Keoni said, "See."

Koa came back carrying the side cutters.

He put them on the bench.

Keoni looked at them.

"Where were they?"

"By the poster stuff."

"Why?"

"Because I forgot."

"Hook."

Koa hung them on the pegboard.

Malia watched.

Normal position.

Not because the hook was visually satisfying.

Because Keoni needed to know the cutters were available before he started a wiring job.

The distinction was small.

It was not cosmetic.

Koa held up the poster frame.

"Now?"

Keoni pointed at the zip ties.

Koa took two.

He repaired the corner at the edge of the bench.

Malia watched him pull the tie too tight.

Keoni said, "Stop."

Koa stopped.

"Too much."

"It's not moving."

"You're bending the frame."

Koa loosened it.

Keoni showed him once.

Not gently.

Not unkindly.

Just enough.

Koa redid it.

Better.

Then he took the side cutters from the hook, clipped the tails, and returned them.

This time without being told.

Keoni nodded.

Koa noticed.

Pretended not to.

Malia noticed that too.

The girl behind Koa looked at a small flat screwdriver on the bench.

"This one yours?"

"Yeah," Keoni said.

"My grandpa has same."

"Everybody's grandpa has same."

She laughed.

Koa picked up his project.

As he turned, his elbow brushed a small tin of screws.

Malia reached.

Keoni said, "Don't."

She froze.

The tin tipped.

Three screws rolled onto the bench.

One fell to the floor.

Keoni watched where it went.

Then picked up the three on the bench.

Koa crouched for the fourth.

"Sorry."

"You're good."

Malia looked at the tin.

Then at Keoni.

"You told me not to catch it."

"Yeah."

"Why?"

"Because you would've knocked the tray."

She looked beside the tin.

The shallow metal tray from Kimo's winch sat half an inch away.

Full of small parts arranged in an order Malia had not realized was order.

If she had lunged, she would have hit it.

Probably.

Maybe.

Keoni saw her looking.

"Current job."

"Right."

Koa left with the poster.

His friend followed.

The workshop quieted again.

Malia stood where she was.

Then she pointed at the metal tray.

"Order."

"Yeah."

"What kind?"

"Disassembly."

She looked closer.

The parts ran left to right.

Washers.

Spacer.

Pawl.

Spring.

Bolt.

Not grouped by type.

Grouped by removal.

"So you can reverse it."

"Yeah."

Malia stared.

At the hotel she would have called the tray wrong.

Mixed hardware.

No labels.

No separation.

Here it was a sentence.

Take apart from right to left.

Put back from left to right.

Or the opposite.

She checked.

"Which direction?"

Keoni pointed.

"Started there."

So reverse.

She nodded.

He smiled.

"Careful."

"What?"

"You're learning."

"Don't ruin it."

Keoni went back to the winch.

Malia moved to the side shelf.

There was an old screwdriver there with a yellowed clear handle.

She recognized it.

Not the exact tool.

The type.

Cheap.

Flathead.

The handle clouded from age.

She said, "You've had that forever."

Keoni glanced over.

"That one? Yeah."

"Since we were kids?"

"Maybe."

Malia touched the shelf beside it, not the tool.

"Nohea used to steal your screwdrivers."

Keoni paused.

Then laughed once.

"Not steal."

"Absolutely steal."

"She'd bring them back."

"To random places."

"Yeah."

A memory moved between them.

Not the storm.

Something smaller.

Keoni said, "She used one to open that stupid plastic horse."

Malia looked at him.

"What horse?"

"Yours."

"I did not have a plastic horse."

"You did."

"I hated horses."

"Maybe that's why she broke it."

Malia stared.

Keoni was smiling now.

"Pink thing. Battery door."

Malia remembered.

Not a horse.

A unicorn.

Cheap white plastic with purple hair.

Nohea had wanted to see how the sound button worked.

Malia had told her no.

Nohea had taken Keoni's screwdriver anyway.

Then returned it inside the kitchen towel drawer.

"She put it with the towels," Malia said.

Keoni laughed.

"Yeah."

"You were so mad."

"Because I looked for it all day."

"She said towels were tools."

Keoni looked at her.

"Did she?"

"Something like that."

"Sounds like her."

Malia smiled.

Then the smile left naturally.

No pressure.

No grief performance.

Just gone.

Keoni picked up the replacement ratchet.

"Hold this."

Malia went over.

"What am I holding?"

"Housing."

"Where?"

"Here."

He showed her.

She held it.

He installed the ratchet.

This time when he asked for the half-inch socket, Malia knew which one.

Not because she had learned the tool bag.

Because Keoni had placed it on the bench for the current job.

She handed it over.

"Right?"

"Yeah."

Small victory.

They worked.

At one point Malia looked toward the pegboard.

The side cutters were on their hook.

The cracked hammer under the window.

Three press wrenches on their separate nail.

Nothing aligned visually.

Everything available.

Keoni tightened the last bolt.

Then tested the ratchet.

Click.

Hold.

Release.

Click.

Hold.

He did it three times.

"Good?"

Malia asked.

"Now."

He marked the housing with a paint pen.

A short blue line.

Malia looked at the blue-taped starter across the room.

"That's how things become blue."

"Sometimes."

"What does the line mean?"

"Rebuilt."

"Write that down."

"No."

She sighed.

Keoni put the winch aside.

Then stopped.

His eyes moved toward the west wall.

Malia followed.

"What?"

He walked to the pegboard.

The side cutters were there.

He ignored them.

His hand went to an empty lower hook.

"What goes there?"

Malia asked.

"Long pliers."

"Did Koa take those too?"

"No."

"How do you know?"

Keoni looked at the bench beneath the hook.

Then the floor.

Then a cardboard box near the side door.

He walked to it.

Inside were old electrical fittings.

He lifted one.

A red crimp connector had been freshly compressed onto a short scrap of wire.

Malia crouched.

"Recent?"

"Yeah."

"How?"

"Bright copper."

He turned the wire.

No darkening.

No dust.

Then he looked at the open side door.

"Aunty Nani."

Malia stared.

"You're joking."

Keoni stood.

"No."

"Why would Aunty Nani be crimping wire in your workshop?"

"Porch light."

Malia remembered.

Too bright after Kalei fixed it.

Keoni took out his phone.

Called.

Put it on speaker.

Aunty Nani answered on the third ring.

"What?"

Keoni said, "You have my long-nose?"

Pause.

"Maybe."

Malia covered her mouth.

Keoni looked at her.

"Aunty."

"I needed pliers."

"Those are not regular pliers."

"They worked."

"Where are they?"

"My kitchen."

Keoni closed his eyes.

"When?"

"Yesterday."

"You came in my shop?"

"Door was open."

"You ask."

"I asked Kalei."

Keoni looked toward the main house.

Malia almost laughed.

"Bring them back."

"I will."

"When?"

"When I come."

"Aunty."

"What?"

"Today."

"Bossy."

She hung up.

Malia laughed.

Keoni put the phone away.

"Human residue."

"Don't."

"You solved it."

"Because she crimps like garbage."

"Still counts."

He put the fresh wire scrap back in the box.

Malia looked at the empty hook.

"If I came in here alone, I would've thought the missing pliers mattered because the hook was empty."

"Okay."

"I would've checked whether anything else moved."

"Okay."

"I might've noticed the wire."

"Maybe."

"But I wouldn't know what it meant."

"No."

She looked around the workshop.

The room had not become less messy.

It had become less hers.

That was different.

Keoni picked up the rebuilt winch.

Malia said, "Normal position depends on the job."

He looked at her.

"Sometimes."

"Person."

"Sometimes."

"Time."

"Sure."

She waited.

Keoni adjusted his grip on the winch.

"You're trying to make one rule again."

Malia sighed.

"Fuck you."

"Better."

He carried the winch toward the door.

Malia followed.

On the way out, she saw the old clear-handled screwdriver on the shelf.

For a second she pictured it in a kitchen towel drawer twenty-four years earlier.

Wrong place.

Right person.

Or maybe the opposite.

She left it where Keoni had put it.
