# The Clear Way

Malia fixed the yard before breakfast.

Not as a test.

She said that out loud once, to herself, then felt stupid and stopped.

The school-project table went back under the lanai.

The archive boxes moved inside against the west wall of the main room where Nalani had finally cleared enough space for them.

The laundry line returned to the usual central post because the morning sun had shifted and Kalei said it no longer mattered.

Keoni's hose stayed coiled by the workshop.

The north-side cookhouse service door stayed closed.

By seven thirty, the yard looked like it had the week before.

Current.

Useful.

Normal.

Koa came outside carrying toast.

He stopped.

"You moved my table."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because your project is done."

"We still need it."

"For what?"

"Presentation boards."

"They're inside."

"Because you moved the table."

Malia looked at him.

Koa looked back.

"Fine," she said. "You need it today?"

"After school."

"Then put it where you need it after school."

"That's what I did yesterday."

"You put it in the middle of the yard."

"Because of wind."

Malia pointed under the lanai.

"Wind's down."

Koa looked at the trees.

Then at her.

"Okay."

No fight.

That should have felt better.

It did not.

Koa took another bite of toast.

Then he walked the current route toward the cookhouse.

Across the middle.

West door.

Malia watched.

Nothing about the old diagonal mattered.

Good.

Kalei came out with a laundry basket.

Malia said, "I put the line back."

"I saw."

"Okay?"

Kalei looked at it.

"Okay."

Malia waited.

Kalei looked at her.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Kalei smiled.

"Careful."

Malia went inside.

For almost an hour, the yard behaved.

Nalani sorted donation bags.

Keoni left to drop off Kimo's rebuilt winch.

Kalei cooked.

Malia cleaned the main room because cleaning was still a task with edges.

Dust.

Sweep.

Wipe.

Done.

No interpretation required.

At eight forty, it started raining.

Not much.

Enough.

Kalei came through the back door.

"Line."

Malia looked outside.

The clothes were already getting spotted.

She followed.

Together they pulled the laundry down.

"Inside?" Malia asked.

"Lanai."

They strung the line under the deeper overhang.

Malia stopped.

The line now ran lengthwise along the lanai edge.

Not across the yard.

Fine.

Better, actually.

Nothing to do with anything.

Kalei rehung towels.

Malia rehung shirts.

The old diagonal remained open.

Malia noticed.

Then deliberately looked at a sock instead.

At nine fifteen, Nalani started carrying donation bags outside.

Malia caught her at the door.

"Where are those going?"

"Car."

"Whose?"

"Aunty Nani's."

"Why?"

"She's taking them."

"When?"

"Later."

Malia looked at the bags.

Four large black bags.

One box.

"Don't leave them in the yard."

Nalani stared.

"I wasn't planning to."

She took the first two under the lanai.

Malia followed.

The rain had stopped again, but the concrete remained wet.

The deepest dry section under the lanai was occupied by laundry.

Nalani moved toward the west side.

Malia said, "Workshop access."

Nalani changed direction.

"Then where?"

There were not many options.

Main-house west wall had the archive boxes inside.

Cookhouse west door needed clear food access.

Center yard wet.

East side sun had returned but the donation bags were black plastic and one box was cardboard.

Nalani chose a narrow dry strip beside the cookhouse's west wall.

Not blocking the door.

Close enough to make the turn tighter.

Malia watched.

Nalani dropped the bags.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Nalani pointed at her.

"No."

"I'm not."

"You're doing math."

"I am literally standing."

"You can do math standing."

Malia looked away.

Nalani brought the remaining bags out.

The current route still worked.

Main house.

Central yard.

Angle west.

Turn around donation bags.

Cookhouse door.

One extra turn.

That was all.

At ten, Keoni came back with a long aluminum frame in the truck bed.

Malia met him by the workshop.

"What is that?"

"Seat frame."

"For?"

"Boat."

"Whose?"

He looked at her.

She stopped.

"Never mind."

"Growth."

He carried one end.

Malia took the other without being asked.

They moved it toward the workshop.

Halfway there, Keoni stopped.

"No room."

Malia looked through the open doors.

The main bench held an outboard housing.

Floor space near the side wall was stacked with scrap Keoni had said he was taking out.

"Where does it go?"

"Outside."

"Rain."

"Under cover."

The workshop overhang was narrow.

The driest place sat between the workshop and cookhouse, exactly where the normal westward yard route widened before the cookhouse door.

Malia looked at him.

"No."

Keoni stared.

"What?"

"Put it somewhere else."

"Why?"

"People walk there."

"People can walk around."

She heard Chapter Three inside that sentence.

Hose.

Walk around.

Malia looked at the frame.

Long.

Awkward.

Heavy enough to need both of them.

"How long?"

"Till I weld it."

"Today?"

"Maybe."

Malia closed her eyes.

Keoni laughed.

"Actual maybe."

They put the frame under the overhang.

It did not block the route.

Not fully.

It narrowed it to a shoulder-width channel between the donation bags and the aluminum.

Malia turned sideways and walked through.

Possible.

Annoying.

Keoni said, "Problem?"

"No."

"You look like problem."

"It's fine."

"It is."

That was the worst part.

It was fine.

At ten thirty, Koa came home early.

Malia looked at the time.

"Why are you here?"

"Teacher workday."

"You had school."

"Half."

"You didn't say."

"I did."

"You said school thing."

"Different day."

Malia stared.

Koa carried a reusable grocery bag and a rolled-up poster.

"What's that?"

"Food."

"For?"

He opened the bag.

Three containers.

"Group."

"You brought leftovers?"

"Other people's leftovers."

Malia looked.

"Why?"

"They didn't want them."

"So you did."

"Free food."

Reasonable.

He walked toward the cookhouse.

Malia watched him approach the current west route.

Donation bags on one side.

Seat frame on the other.

He stopped.

Not for long.

Then he turned.

Diagonal.

Across the yard.

Toward the north service door.

Malia said, "Koa."

He turned.

"What?"

"Use the front."

He looked at the west door.

Then at the bags.

Then the aluminum frame.

"Why?"

Malia heard the question.

Not suspicious.

Not defiant.

Why take the harder way?

"Because that's the kitchen entrance."

"Cookhouse."

"Whatever."

Koa looked at her.

"You want me to squeeze through there carrying this?"

"It's not blocked."

He held up the bag.

One container tilted.

Malia saw sauce shift under the lid.

The answer was obvious.

"No."

Koa kept walking diagonal.

North door.

Gone.

Malia stood in the yard.

Kalei came out carrying a towel.

"You told him to use the west door?"

Malia looked at her.

"It's the normal door."

"Right now it's stupid."

Malia almost argued.

Did not.

Kalei hung the towel under the lanai.

"Move the bags if you care."

Malia looked at the donation bags.

Then the seat frame.

She could.

She absolutely could.

That would be ordinary work.

Not a test.

She walked to the bags.

Grabbed one.

Stopped.

Where?

Aunty Nani's car was not here yet.

The main room had no space.

The workshop access needed to stay open.

The yard was wet.

The cookhouse east side had food containers waiting to go home.

Every available position created another problem.

Malia set the bag back.

Kalei watched.

"Yeah."

"Don't."

"I didn't say anything."

"Your face did."

Kalei smiled.

"Family disease."

Malia went inside.

At eleven fifteen, Aunty Nani arrived.

Her car stayed outside the gate because somebody else was parked along the road.

She came in carrying nothing.

Nalani pointed toward the donation bags.

"Those."

Aunty Nani looked.

"Later."

"You said you were taking them."

"I am."

"When?"

"When I leave."

"You just got here."

"Exactly."

Nalani stared.

Aunty Nani walked toward the cookhouse.

Current route.

She reached the narrowed section.

Stopped.

"Who put this here?"

Keoni, from the workshop, said, "Me."

"Bad place."

"Temporary."

Aunty Nani stepped over the corner of one donation bag.

Her slipper caught the plastic.

The bag tipped.

Nothing fell out.

Still.

She swore.

Malia moved.

"You okay?"

"I'm fine."

Aunty Nani kicked the bag upright.

"Move this."

Nalani said, "Where?"

Aunty Nani pointed vaguely.

"Somewhere."

Keoni came over.

Looked at the route.

Looked at the frame.

"Yeah. Too tight."

Malia felt something cold and stupid in her stomach.

Keoni took two donation bags.

"Put these by the main-house east wall."

"Sun," Nalani said.

"They're clothes."

"One box is paper."

"Bag the box."

Nalani looked offended by the inelegance.

Then did it.

They moved the donation bags.

Malia watched the west route reopen.

Good.

Current again.

The aluminum frame still narrowed it, but not badly.

Aunty Nani walked through.

"Better."

She entered by the west door.

Malia exhaled.

Nothing had won.

Nothing was choosing.

People changed problems when they noticed them.

That was normal too.

At noon, they ate.

Koa's containers became part of lunch.

One had noodles.

One had fried rice.

One had something so spicy Nalani coughed after one bite and accused Koa of attempted murder.

He ate it happily.

Aunty Nani left with two donation bags.

Not all of them.

Of course.

Keoni ate fast and went back to the frame.

Malia did not look at the yard for almost forty minutes.

Then Nalani asked her to help move the last donation box into Aunty Nani's car.

They took it outside.

The west route was blocked again.

Not by the bags.

By a folding chair.

Malia stopped.

"Why is that there?"

Nalani looked.

"Chair?"

"Yes."

"No idea."

Koa called from the lanai.

"Mine."

Malia turned.

"Why?"

"Glue."

She stared.

He held up the poster.

One corner had separated again.

He had moved a chair beside the west cookhouse door because that section of overhang was dry and out of the wind.

On the chair sat glue, tape, a ruler, and two clamps borrowed from Keoni.

The seat frame still took the workshop side.

The chair took the cookhouse side.

Current route:

possible.

Barely.

Malia looked at Koa.

"Move it."

"Where?"

"Lanai."

"Wind."

"Inside."

"Glue smell."

"Cookhouse."

"Kalei said no."

From inside, Kalei shouted, "Correct."

Nalani laughed.

Malia did not.

Koa looked at her.

"You want me to move it for no reason."

"There is a reason."

"What?"

She stopped.

Because that's not where things go.

The answer arrived cleanly.

Lehua-clean.

Malia heard it before she said it.

She did not say it.

Instead:

"People are walking there."

Koa pointed across the yard.

"Walk there."

The old route.

Open.

Again.

Malia looked.

Laundry under lanai.

Donation bags mostly gone.

Seat frame west.

Chair west.

Hose coiled.

Central yard damp but clear.

Diagonal untouched.

Nalani followed her gaze.

"Oh."

Koa looked between them.

"What?"

Malia took the box from Nalani.

"Nothing."

Koa groaned.

"Stop saying that."

Fair.

They carried the box to the gate using the current main-house exit, not the old diagonal because the car was makai.

When they returned, Malia deliberately used the west cookhouse route.

She turned sideways around the chair.

The cardboard box wasn't there anymore, but the clamps stuck out.

One caught the hem of her shirt.

Not hard.

Enough.

She stopped.

Freed it.

Koa watched.

"See?"

"Don't."

She stepped around the seat frame.

Entered through the west door.

Inside, Kalei was portioning leftovers into containers.

She looked at Malia's shirt.

"You tear it?"

"No."

"Then you're fine."

Malia stared at her.

Kalei stared back.

Malia laughed despite herself.

"Everybody in this family is terrible."

"Current normal."

That almost made Malia stop.

Kalei did not seem to notice the phrase.

Or maybe she did and chose not to.

Malia helped pack food.

At one thirty, rain started again.

Short.

Harder than the morning.

Kalei looked toward the laundry.

"Shit."

Everyone moved.

Malia ran outside with her.

Koa grabbed one end of the line.

Nalani took shirts.

Aunty Nani, still somehow not gone, took towels.

They stripped the line fast.

Keoni came around the workshop with a tarp.

The yard became bodies and wet fabric.

Nobody thought about routes.

They just moved.

Malia carried an armful of sheets toward the main house.

The current path was blocked by people.

Koa was near the center.

Nalani at the lanai steps.

Keoni crossing from workshop.

The old diagonal was clear.

Malia took it.

Three steps before she realized.

Main house to north side cookhouse was the other direction, but the diagonal itself cut the crowd.

She followed it halfway, then angled back toward the main house.

No meaning.

Crowd flow.

That was all.

Inside, she dumped the sheets on a chair.

Her pulse felt too fast.

Rain stopped eight minutes later.

The family place rearranged again.

Wet laundry stayed under lanai.

Keoni folded the tarp and placed it near the workshop.

Koa moved his glue chair farther under the cookhouse overhang.

Nalani returned to paperwork.

Aunty Nani finally left.

Malia went outside.

She stood at the main-house threshold.

Current route:

central yard.

West cookhouse door.

Chair near overhang.

Seat frame along workshop side.

Laundry crowding lanai.

Still usable.

Old route:

diagonal.

Open.

No object marked it.

Nothing had moved onto it.

Again, everything had moved away.

Malia did not call anyone.

She did not ask Kalei whether she remembered.

She did not get Keoni to measure.

She already knew the answer.

That worried her too.

At three, Koa came out carrying an empty plastic container.

He was looking at his phone.

Not at the yard.

Malia watched.

He walked.

Not toward the west door.

Diagonal.

North service entrance.

No hesitation.

No historical awareness.

No glance at Malia.

Just the clear way.

"Koa."

He stopped with one hand on the service-door handle.

"What?"

Malia looked at the current route.

Then at him.

"Why that door?"

He looked behind himself.

Then toward the west side.

"Because it's easier."

"Again."

"Yeah."

"Doesn't that feel weird?"

Koa stared at her.

"No."

She waited.

He shrugged.

"You guys are the ones who made the other way stupid."

Then he went inside.

Malia stayed in the yard.

That sentence landed harder than anything else had.

You guys made the other way stupid.

Not the family place.

Not the dead.

Not a force.

Them.

Living people.

Current decisions.

Separate reasons.

Same result.

Keoni came out of the workshop carrying a welding helmet.

Malia looked at him.

He saw Koa disappearing through the north door.

Then he looked at the west route.

Chair.

Seat frame.

Laundry.

He said nothing.

Malia waited.

Keoni put on the helmet.

She said, "You see it."

"Yeah."

"Again."

"Yeah."

"Four different people."

"More."

Malia looked at him.

"Kalei moved laundry."

"Rain."

"Koa moved chair."

"Glue."

"You put the frame there."

"No room inside."

"Nalani staged bags there."

"Then moved them."

"Exactly."

Keoni lifted the helmet again.

"What do you want me to say?"

Malia looked at the diagonal.

The north service door stood open now because Koa had left it that way.

She could see a strip of cookhouse floor through it.

"I don't know."

Keoni followed her gaze.

Then he said, "It is easier."

"That's all?"

"That's the thing I know."

Malia nodded.

Fair.

Materially fair.

Keoni lowered the helmet and went back to work.

Malia stood alone.

She could restore the yard again.

Move the chair.

Ask Keoni to shift the frame.

Rehang laundry somewhere else.

Close the north door.

Nothing stopped her.

That mattered.

She did none of it.

Not because she wanted evidence.

She hoped that was true.

Because the family place was being used.

Koa needed the chair.

Keoni needed the dry work space.

Kalei needed the laundry covered.

The north door was allowed to be a door.

Every present need was legitimate.

That was what made the old route worse.

It did not have to defeat the present.

The present kept making room for it.
