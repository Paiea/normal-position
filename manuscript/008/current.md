# Throw It Away

Nalani found the slippers under Tūtū Lehua's chair.

Not hidden.

Not forgotten.

Just where they had always been.

One on its side.

One upright.

Gray rubber gone almost white across the straps. The heel cups flattened smooth from years of wear. On the left slipper, the outer strap had begun to split where it entered the sole.

Nalani held them by two fingers.

"Trash."

Kalei looked up from the dresser.

"No."

Nalani stopped.

Malia, sitting on the floor with a stack of folded towels beside her, looked between them.

The room had been easier that morning.

Not easy.

Easier.

They had finished the dresser.

They had separated Kalei's sewing kits from Lehua's things. Koa's charger was already gone. Old medication packaging had been bagged for disposal. Two sweaters had moved into a keep pile because Nalani remembered buying one and Kalei remembered Lehua refusing to wear the other unless the morning was actually cold.

No system survived contact with both sisters.

Still, they were moving.

Then the slippers.

Nalani looked at Kalei.

"They're broken."

"The strap is splitting."

"That's broken."

"It still holds."

"For what?"

Kalei did not answer.

Nalani lifted the slippers slightly.

"For what?"

Malia watched Kalei's face.

Nothing dramatic.

That made the answer easier to hear when it came.

"I put those on her."

Nalani's arm lowered.

Malia looked at the chair.

Tūtū Lehua's chair sat beside the bed, wooden arms polished darker where hands had rested. The slippers had apparently lived beneath it when Lehua wasn't wearing them.

Malia could see the routine without needing it explained.

Lehua sitting.

Kalei crouching.

One foot.

Then the other.

A strap guided over skin that had become easier to bruise.

Nalani looked at the slippers.

Then at Kalei.

"I know."

Kalei's mouth tightened.

"Do you?"

"Yes."

"You weren't here."

There it was.

Not loud.

Not new.

Nalani set the slippers on the bed instead of throwing them into the trash bag.

Malia felt herself exhale.

That irritated her.

She had not realized she was waiting.

Nalani rubbed one hand over her forehead.

"I know I wasn't here."

Kalei went back to the dresser.

"No, you know the sentence."

Nalani stared.

Malia stood.

"Maybe we don't need to do this right now."

Both sisters looked at her.

Wrong move.

Malia immediately knew it.

Kalei said, "We're doing it now."

Nalani said, "Apparently."

Malia looked at the slippers on the bed.

Gray rubber.

Split strap.

Worn heel.

Nothing to mediate.

That helped.

"Are they useful?" Malia asked.

Nalani answered, "No."

Kalei answered, "They were."

Malia nodded.

"Different question."

Kalei looked at her.

Malia pointed.

"Would anyone wear them?"

"No," Kalei said.

"Would you repair them?"

Kalei looked at the split strap.

Longer this time.

"No."

"Do you want to keep them?"

Kalei did not answer immediately.

Nalani waited.

That mattered.

Finally Kalei said, "No."

Nalani's shoulders dropped.

Not relief exactly.

Permission.

Malia picked up one slipper.

The rubber was warm from the room.

She turned it over.

The heel had worn unevenly, more on the outside edge.

"She walked like that at the end?"

Kalei nodded.

"Right knee."

"Pain?"

"Sometimes."

"Why not replace them?"

Kalei gave her a look.

"We did."

Malia looked at the slipper.

Kalei said, "She wore the new ones twice."

"Why?"

"Said these didn't slap the floor."

Nalani laughed once.

Malia looked at her.

Nalani covered her mouth.

"Sorry."

Kalei's face almost softened.

"She hated that sound."

Malia remembered.

Not the slippers.

The sound.

Lehua's irritation with noise had been specific.

Cup set too hard on a table.

Cabinet closed from six inches instead of one.

Slippers slapping tile.

Malia smiled despite herself.

"She would've hated Koa."

Kalei said, "She did."

Nalani looked offended.

"No, she didn't."

"At seven in the morning? Absolutely."

Malia laughed.

There.

A little room opened around the object.

Not sacred.

Not meaningless.

Just used.

Nalani looked at the slippers again.

"Trash?"

Kalei stared at them.

Then nodded.

"Trash."

Nalani picked them up.

This time Kalei did not stop her.

Malia watched Nalani put them into the black trash bag.

The bag already held medication wrappers, two cracked plastic hangers, expired lotion, a dead remote, and three pairs of socks Kalei said had lost elasticity before Lehua lost patience with them.

The slippers landed on top.

Soft sound.

Nothing else.

Nalani tied the bag.

Malia's eyes went to the floor beside the chair.

Two pale outlines in the dust where the slippers had rested.

She hated that immediately.

Not because it was eerie.

Because it made the absence legible.

Kalei saw her looking.

"Wipe under there."

Malia looked up.

"What?"

"Before you put anything else back."

"Right."

She got the cloth.

The pale outlines disappeared.

That should have helped.

It did not.

Outside, Keoni had backed his truck near the workshop.

The bed already held two bags of ordinary household trash and one cardboard box of broken things Nalani had declared not worth discussing individually.

The slippers went into the third bag.

Malia carried it out.

Keoni looked over.

"You done with that room?"

"No."

"Then why are you carrying trash like you've won?"

"Because apparently that's what progress looks like."

He opened the truck bed.

Malia put the bag in.

"Dump run?"

"Eventually."

"When?"

Keoni stared at her.

Malia heard the question the way he probably did.

Too interested.

She changed it.

"You taking anything else?"

"Workshop scrap. Maybe old catchment hose."

"Today?"

"Maybe."

Malia closed her eyes.

Keoni smiled.

"Good talk."

She walked away.

The slippers were in the bag.

The bag was in the truck.

That was enough.

She told herself so.

At lunch, she looked through the workshop window at the truck bed.

The bag was still there.

She hated herself a little.

At two, Keoni drove off.

Malia heard the truck leave.

She was in the cookhouse washing a knife.

She stopped with the blade under running water.

Kalei noticed.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Kalei looked through the window.

The truck was gone.

Then she looked back at Malia.

"Oh."

Malia kept washing the knife.

Kalei dried a bowl.

"You waiting for the slippers?"

"No."

"Mm."

"I'm not."

"Okay."

Malia turned off the water.

"Do you think they'll come back?"

Kalei set the bowl down.

"No."

The answer came too easily.

Malia looked at her.

"No?"

"They're slippers."

"So was the cup."

"The cup never left."

"The key did."

"People use keys."

"The shelf."

Kalei took another bowl.

"People use shelves."

Malia leaned against the counter.

"You don't think any of it is strange?"

Kalei dried slowly.

"I think you're looking very hard."

"That's not an answer."

"It is one."

Malia hated that too.

Kalei put the bowl away.

Then she looked at her.

"I think the shelf was strange."

Malia waited.

"I think the key was not."

"Okay."

"And I think you want all of it to be the same thing."

Malia straightened.

"No, I don't."

"You do."

"Why would I want that?"

Kalei did not answer.

Malia felt the answer arrive anyway.

Because then the pattern was real.

Because then she was not being ridiculous.

Because then every small wrong thing she had noticed belonged to something larger.

Because uncertainty was exhausting.

She looked at the lower shelf.

Current normal.

No blue cup high.

No old arrangement.

Just cups.

Kalei said, "You want one answer."

"I want the right answer."

"Same problem."

Malia opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Kalei went back to work.

The afternoon became ordinary on purpose.

Not because anyone was trying.

Because ordinary things kept requiring attention.

Koa needed a ride.

Nalani found a property-tax notice mixed into old medical papers and spent twenty minutes trying to determine whether it had already been paid.

Then the notice became everybody's problem.

She carried it into the cookhouse.

"Kalei."

Kalei was cutting green onion.

"What?"

"Was this paid?"

Kalei looked at the paper from six feet away.

"Which one?"

"Property tax."

"What year?"

Nalani looked.

"This year."

"Probably."

Nalani shut her eyes.

Malia, drying dishes, felt sympathy for both of them.

"Probably is not a payment method," Nalani said.

"I know."

"Then where's the receipt?"

"Check the blue folder."

"Which blue folder?"

"The house one."

"There are four blue folders."

"Big one."

"Two are big."

Kalei put down the knife.

"You want me to stop cooking?"

"No."

"Then ask Keoni."

"Why would Keoni know?"

"Because he paid online last time."

Nalani stared.

"With whose money?"

Kalei stared back.

"Does that matter if you're asking whether it got paid?"

"Yes."

"Then ask a different question."

Malia put the dish towel down.

This family had become intolerably good at telling people to ask different questions.

Nalani held up the notice.

"Who has been paying for this place?"

Kalei returned to the green onion.

"Depends."

"On what?"

"Which thing."

"Property tax."

"Sometimes me. Sometimes Mom. Keoni did last year because the card wasn't working."

"Insurance?"

Kalei shrugged.

"Mom."

"Utilities?"

"Me mostly."

"Catchment?"

"That's not one bill."

Nalani looked at Malia.

Malia immediately looked away.

Kalei continued.

"Pump was Keoni. Filters are whoever buys them. Roof cleaning, us. Tank cleaning, we hired last time. Gutters, Keoni. Electrical work depends what broke."

Nalani put the notice flat on the counter.

"Why is none of this written down?"

"It is."

"Where?"

Kalei pointed vaguely toward the main house.

Malia said, "Please don't say blue folder."

Kalei almost smiled.

"Green notebook."

Nalani made a sound through her nose.

Malia dried another plate.

The slippers remained at the dump.

Somehow that thought had attached itself to everything.

Nalani said, "This is exactly why we need to decide what happens with the property."

Kalei's knife stopped.

Not dramatically.

Just stopped.

Malia watched.

Nalani saw it too.

"I didn't say sell."

"You thought it."

"Thinking isn't paperwork."

"You make paperwork out of everything."

"I am trying to find out what this costs."

"It costs money."

"Helpful."

"It costs work too."

Nalani leaned against the counter.

"I know that."

"No. You know the sentence."

The same line Kalei had used in Lehua's room.

Nalani's face hardened.

Malia stepped in before either woman could sharpen it further.

"What work isn't being counted?"

Kalei looked at her.

Malia nodded toward the property-tax notice.

"If we're going to talk cost, then what are we missing?"

Kalei wiped the knife.

"Keoni checks the gutters. Fixes the workshop. Keeps the pump alive. Drives Mom when I can't. Koa cuts grass when you can make him. I do the bills, food, appointments, laundry, whatever needs doing."

Nalani said, "You shouldn't have had to do all of it."

Kalei looked at her.

That was not the answer she had expected.

Nalani looked down at the notice.

"I mean that."

Kalei's shoulders changed.

Barely.

Still.

Malia saw it.

Nalani continued.

"But that also doesn't mean nobody gets to ask what happens next."

"I didn't say that."

"You act like every question is an accusation."

"Because most of yours arrive with a deadline."

Nalani folded the notice once.

Then unfolded it because the crease bothered her.

Malia almost laughed.

Did not.

Nalani said, "Fine. No deadline today."

Kalei picked up the knife again.

"Good."

"Tomorrow, maybe."

Kalei looked up.

Nalani smiled.

Small.

Not kind exactly.

Familiar.

Kalei shook her head and kept cutting.

Malia picked up the dish towel.

No resolution.

Still, the room had changed.

The property was not one thing either.

Not asset.

Not burden.

Not inheritance.

Not home.

It was bills and gutters and old grief and somebody remembering to buy filters before the old one failed.

It was Keoni paying online because a card did not work.

It was Kalei knowing which folder mattered.

It was Nalani being allowed to ask whether any of that could keep going.

Malia dried the plate and put it away.

Current shelf.

Current house.

Current problems.

Aunty Nani came by for a casserole dish and stayed long enough to complain about her porch light, which Kalei had fixed but which she now considered too bright.

Keoni returned without the truck bed trash.

Malia noticed immediately.

Of course she did.

She waited until he came inside.

"Dump?"

"Yeah."

"All of it?"

Keoni looked at her.

Malia regretted the wording before he answered.

"All what?"

"The trash."

"That's generally how the dump works."

"Kalei had some things in there."

"Then they're gone."

Simple.

Malia looked toward Tūtū Lehua's room.

Keoni followed her eyes.

"You're doing something weird again."

"I'm not."

"Okay."

He took a metal cup from the middle shelf, filled it, and left.

Malia went to the room.

The place beside the chair was empty.

Not suspiciously empty.

Just empty.

She crouched.

Nothing under the bed.

Nothing behind the chair.

Nothing in the laundry basket.

The comb sat in its dish.

The slippers did not.

Malia stood.

She told herself that was good.

At dinner, the slippers were still gone.

At sunset, gone.

After the front door was locked and the key returned to its hook, gone.

Malia brushed her teeth.

Checked the room again.

Gone.

She went to bed.

Her travel bag was still under the bed.

The house settled around her in ordinary sounds.

Water moving somewhere in the pipes.

A chair scraping once in the main room.

Koa's voice low through a wall.

Rain beginning again, lighter this time.

Malia lay awake.

Not waiting.

She told herself that too.

She thought about the slippers in the trash bag.

Then the truck.

Then the dump.

She imagined the split strap under other garbage.

The worn heels.

The gray rubber.

Nothing special.

Nothing returning.

She fell asleep annoyed.

In the morning, she checked the chair before she checked the key.

The floor was empty.

No slippers.

No substitute.

No strange object in their place.

The comb remained where it belonged.

The room was simply missing something it no longer needed.

Malia stood in the doorway.

Relief came first.

Then disappointment.

That was worse.
