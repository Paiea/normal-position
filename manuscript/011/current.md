# The Old Path

The yard looked better after rain.

That was Malia's first mistake.

Not cleaner.

Better.

The mud had settled.

The concrete under the lanai had dried in patches.

The grass along the workshop side was flattened where people had crossed it wet.

Nothing had moved overnight that she could see.

No cup.

No key.

No comb.

No slippers.

She had checked all four before breakfast and hated herself less than she expected.

Progress.

Then Kalei told her to move the laundry.

"Where?"

Kalei pointed with two clothespins between her teeth.

The line ran from one lanai post toward a metal pole near the center of the yard. Shirts, towels, and two bedsheets hung across it in a broad pale wall.

"Other side."

Malia looked.

"Why?"

Kalei removed the clothespins.

"Sun."

The answer was obvious once she looked.

The cloud cover had broken unevenly. The east side of the yard had light. The current line sat mostly in shade.

Malia unhooked one end.

Koa came through carrying a backpack.

"Need help?"

"No."

He kept walking.

"That wasn't sincere."

"I asked."

"You were already leaving."

"Still counts."

He went through the gate.

Malia dragged the laundry line toward the east post.

Kalei helped lift the sheets clear of a folding table.

They rehung the line closer to the cookhouse wall.

The towels caught the light.

Better.

Kalei went inside.

Malia stood with one hand on the line.

The middle of the yard had opened.

Not all the way.

Just more than before.

She noticed because open space was easier to clean.

That was all.

She went to get the broom.

By nine, Keoni had a hose stretched from the workshop toward the catchment tank.

The hose crossed the yard at ankle height.

Malia stopped.

"That's terrible."

Keoni was under the tank housing.

"What?"

"This."

She pointed.

"Temporary."

"People are walking here."

"Walk around."

"That's not a system."

"It's a hose."

Malia looked toward the cookhouse.

The west-facing main door was open.

The usual route from the house cut through the central yard, around the table, then west into the cookhouse.

The hose now crossed part of that route.

She stepped over it.

"How long?"

"Ten minutes."

"Actual ten?"

Keoni looked at her.

"Go away."

She did.

Twelve minutes later, he finished.

Malia knew because she heard the water stop.

She was inside the main house with Nalani, sorting a stack of envelopes into keep, shred, and ask-Kalei.

Nalani held up a property-insurance notice.

"Ask?"

"Obviously."

"Everything is ask."

"Because nobody knows."

"That is not how estates are supposed to work."

"Tell the estate."

Nalani put the notice into the ask pile.

Outside, something scraped across concrete.

Malia looked toward the door.

Stopped herself.

Nalani noticed.

"You're trying not to look."

"No."

"You are."

Malia kept sorting.

"Growth."

"Terrifying."

Five minutes later, Keoni came inside for water.

Malia glanced through the doorway.

The hose was no longer across the central yard.

He had coiled it against the workshop side.

Not neatly.

Functionally.

The usual route to the cookhouse had reopened.

Mostly.

Except Koa's folding table was there now.

Malia stared.

The school-project table had been moved from under the lanai into the yard, closer to the cookhouse.

Poster board covered half of it.

One of Koa's friends sat on a chair trimming printed photos.

Malia stepped outside.

"Koa."

He looked up.

"What?"

"Why is this here?"

"Glue."

She waited.

He pointed toward the lanai.

"Too windy."

The lanai was catching crosswind.

The table now sat lower between the cookhouse and main house, partly sheltered by both.

Reasonable.

It blocked the normal westward approach to the cookhouse just enough that anyone carrying food would have to angle around it.

Malia looked at the table.

Then the hose.

Then the laundry.

No.

She went back inside.

By ten, Aunty Nani arrived with a cooler.

She came through the gate with both hands occupied and called for somebody before she reached the lanai.

Kalei answered from the cookhouse.

"Bring it inside."

"Heavy."

"I know."

Aunty Nani kept walking.

She did not take the usual route.

The school table blocked most of it.

Instead she went diagonally across the open yard, cut behind the laundry line, and stopped at the north side of the cookhouse.

Malia watched through the main-house window.

Aunty Nani put the cooler down beside the narrow service door.

Not inside.

Beside.

She opened the service door.

Kalei appeared on the other side.

"What is all this?"

"Fish."

"Kimo?"

"No. My nephew."

"You have too much."

"Then eat."

Kalei took one end.

Together they moved the cooler inside.

Malia stood at the window.

The diagonal Aunty Nani had taken stayed in her eyes after the cooler disappeared.

Main house.

Open yard.

North side of cookhouse.

She knew that line.

Not from this week.

Older.

She looked away.

Nalani held up an envelope.

"Malia."

"What?"

"Do we need this?"

Malia looked.

Electric bill.

Six years old.

"No."

Nalani put it in shred.

Malia looked through the window again.

The line was still open.

Of course it was.

Open space tended to remain open until someone used it.

She went back to work.

At eleven, Nalani carried two archive boxes outside because the main room was getting crowded.

Malia followed.

"Where are those going?"

"Lanai."

"It's damp."

"Then where?"

Malia pointed beside the west wall of the main house under the deeper overhang.

Nalani shook her head.

"That's where Kalei keeps the donation bags."

"Other side."

"Sun."

Malia looked.

Too much direct light.

Nalani sighed.

Then carried the boxes toward the yard.

The school table occupied the center.

The laundry occupied the east.

Workshop access stayed busy on the west.

Nalani chose the only dry, shaded patch near the main house that did not obstruct anyone.

She stacked the boxes against the mauka-side corner of the lanai.

Malia watched.

The boxes did not block the diagonal.

They narrowed the current central route instead.

Nalani saw her looking.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Nalani gave her a long look.

Malia corrected herself.

"Good there."

"High praise."

They went back inside.

At noon the family ate leftovers.

Koa's friends stayed.

Aunty Nani stayed.

Keoni ate standing because somebody was supposed to bring back his pliers and had not.

Aunty Nani pointed her fork at him.

"They're in my car."

Keoni stared.

"Why?"

"So I don't forget."

"You forgot them in your car."

"I remembered where."

"Different problem."

Koa laughed.

Malia did too.

For twenty minutes, the yard disappeared as a question.

Then Kalei asked Koa to take an empty food container to the cookhouse.

Koa picked it up.

He stepped outside.

Malia watched without deciding to.

He reached the school table.

Went around it.

Not west.

Diagonal.

Across the open strip.

Past the edge of the shifted laundry.

Straight to the north service door.

He opened it with one hand and disappeared inside.

Malia stopped chewing.

Keoni noticed.

"What?"

She looked at him.

"Did you see how he went?"

Keoni looked toward the yard.

"Koa?"

"Yeah."

"Walked."

"I know."

Nalani set down her fork.

"What?"

Malia pointed.

"He used the north door."

Kalei shrugged.

"Closer from there."

"Is it?"

Everyone looked outside.

The school table blocked the ordinary line.

Laundry narrowed the eastern side.

The central path was usable but not direct.

The diagonal was clear.

Keoni said, "Right now, yeah."

Malia looked at Kalei.

"Did we used to go that way?"

Kalei's face changed.

Small.

She followed the line from the main house toward the cookhouse.

"Long time ago."

Nalani looked too.

"The old kitchen."

Malia felt something tighten below her ribs.

Koa came back through the north door carrying nothing.

He crossed the same diagonal.

"What?"

Malia looked at him.

"Nothing."

He stopped.

"Why is everybody staring?"

Nalani said, "You took the old way."

Koa looked behind him.

"What old way?"

"To the kitchen."

"Cookhouse," Kalei said automatically.

Nalani ignored her.

Koa looked at the yard.

"I took the clear way."

No one answered.

Koa frowned.

"Okay."

He sat down.

Malia finished lunch.

Or moved food around until lunch ended.

Afterward she went outside alone.

She stood at the main-house threshold.

Current route first.

From the front / lanai area into the central yard.

Angle west around the table.

Approach the cookhouse main door.

That was how everyone had been moving all week.

It was how Malia had learned the place now.

Then the other route.

Not marked.

Not straight enough to be designed.

Just available.

From the main house across the yard on a diagonal.

Between the stacked archive boxes and the shifted laundry.

Past the end of the school table.

Clear of the hose coil.

Toward the north service door.

Malia did not walk it.

Not yet.

She went to find Kalei.

Her aunt was inside Tūtū Lehua's room folding two blouses into a donation box.

"Old way."

Kalei did not look up.

"What about it?"

"How old?"

Kalei folded another blouse.

"Depends."

Malia waited.

Kalei sighed.

"Before the cookhouse rebuild, we used that side more."

"Why?"

"Door."

"There was a door there."

"More or less."

"The current service door?"

"Current one is farther east."

Malia tried to picture it.

"How much farther?"

Kalei held up a hand.

"Don't make me survey twenty-four years ago."

"I'm asking."

"I know."

Malia leaned against the dresser, then remembered where she was and straightened.

Kalei noticed.

Did not comment.

"The old kitchen sat a little different," Kalei said. "Same area. Not same walls. We cut across because that was the closest side from the house."

"Everyone?"

"Mostly."

"Nohea?"

Kalei looked at her.

Malia regretted asking immediately.

Not because the question was forbidden.

Because she had made the route about Nohea before it needed to be.

Kalei answered anyway.

"Yes."

Malia looked down.

"That's the way she took."

Kalei was quiet.

Then:

"Probably."

Probably.

Not a revelation.

Not a recovered truth.

A child leaving the main house for the cookhouse would have used the route everyone used.

Malia nodded.

Kalei put the blouse in the donation box.

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Turn every old thing into that night."

Malia looked at her.

Kalei closed the box.

"The path existed before she died."

"I know."

"Then remember that."

Malia left.

She found Nalani at the main table.

"Do you remember the old kitchen route?"

Nalani kept reading a bank statement.

"Yes."

"How?"

Nalani looked up.

"How what?"

"Where it went."

"Across the yard."

"To which side?"

"North-ish."

"Was the old door there?"

"I don't remember the exact door."

"Kalei does."

"Good for Kalei."

Malia sat.

Nalani watched her.

"You think this is another shelf."

Malia did not answer.

Nalani put down the statement.

"Is it?"

"I don't know."

"That's an improvement."

Malia almost smiled.

"Everybody moved something today."

"Everybody always moves something."

"Yeah."

"Kalei moved laundry because of sun."

"Okay."

"Keoni moved the hose."

"Because he finished."

"Koa moved the table."

"Because of wind."

"You moved the boxes."

"Because you told me not to put them anywhere useful."

Malia looked at her.

Nalani smiled faintly.

"Aunty Nani carried the cooler through the north door."

"Because the table was in the way."

"Exactly."

Malia spread her hands.

Nalani looked toward the yard.

"So?"

"So all of those decisions cleared one route."

Nalani followed the line with her eyes.

For a moment, she said nothing.

Then:

"Huh."

Malia hated how much that one syllable affected her.

"You see it."

"I see a clear path."

"An old path."

"Also a clear path."

"Both."

"Maybe."

Malia stood.

"I need Keoni."

Nalani went back to the bank statement.

"Of course you do."

Keoni was in the workshop with the rebuilt winch.

Aunty Nani's long-nose pliers had finally returned.

They hung on the lower hook.

Malia noticed.

Then remembered Chapter Ten's lesson and did not congratulate herself.

"Come look at something."

Keoni did not move.

"What?"

"The yard."

"I can see the yard."

"From outside."

He looked at her.

Then at the winch.

Then back.

"Thirty seconds."

"Fine."

He followed.

Malia took him to the main-house threshold.

"Current route."

She pointed.

"Old route."

She pointed again.

Keoni looked.

"Okay."

"Don't 'okay' me."

"What do you want?"

"Is the old one actually clearer?"

He studied the yard.

That was why she had asked him.

Not because he would believe her.

Because he would measure without measuring.

His eyes went to the table.

Laundry.

Boxes.

Hose coil.

Cookhouse doors.

He walked halfway into the yard.

Stopped.

Turned.

Then walked back.

"Yeah."

Malia's skin tightened.

"Why?"

"Fewer turns."

"Today."

"Today."

"Normally?"

"West door."

"Could the route have happened accidentally?"

Keoni looked at her.

"Obviously."

"I know."

"Then why ask?"

"Because I need you to say it."

He frowned.

Malia pointed to each thing.

"Kalei moved the laundry."

"Sun."

"You moved the hose."

"Finished."

"Koa moved the table."

"Wind."

"Nalani moved boxes."

"Dry."

"Nani used the north door."

"Table."

"Nobody made the route."

"No."

Keoni looked along it again.

Then at Malia.

"But?"

She waited.

He looked back at the diagonal.

"But that's a lot of same direction."

There.

Not belief.

Not proof.

Recognition.

Malia felt worse immediately.

Keoni noticed.

"You wanted me to say no."

"I don't know what I wanted."

"That's honest."

"Don't get used to it."

He returned to the workshop.

Malia stayed.

The yard was busy again.

Koa's friend crossed toward the gate.

Kalei carried folded laundry inside.

Nalani came out with one empty archive box.

Nothing held still for her.

That made the route harder to see.

Then easier.

Because the space remained.

Objects moved around it.

People crossed it.

Nobody had marked it.

Nobody had agreed.

Malia walked to the main-house threshold.

Stopped.

She could take the current route.

Around the table.

West door.

Normal.

Instead she stepped onto the diagonal.

Not as a test.

She told herself that once.

Then did not repeat it.

Across the yard.

Past the stacked boxes.

The air smelled of sun-warmed laundry and damp grass.

Past the end of Koa's table.

A dried streak of glue marked one corner.

Past the hose coiled against the workshop side.

Nothing underfoot but ordinary concrete, grass, and packed earth.

Twenty-four years disappeared badly.

Not gone.

Not present either.

Just layered.

She looked down for evidence and found none.

No worn groove in the grass.

No line of exposed soil.

No old stones showing through.

The yard had been mowed, repaired, crossed, parked on, dried out, soaked, and used by too many people for too many years to preserve one childhood route like a diagram.

If she had come outside yesterday looking for the old path, she would not have found it.

That mattered.

Nothing buried had surfaced.

Nothing hidden had been uncovered.

The route existed now because the things that usually interrupted it were somewhere else.

Malia kept walking.

She did not picture Nohea.

That mattered.

This route had carried groceries.

Laundry.

Cups.

People late for meals.

Keoni at thirteen.

Malia at ten.

Nalani younger than Malia was now.

Kalei before caregiving became the shape of her life.

Tūtū Lehua before grief sharpened every household rule.

Nohea too.

Yes.

But not only Nohea.

The path had belonged to use.

That made the current clearing worse.

Malia reached the north side of the cookhouse.

The service door stood open.

Inside, Kalei was putting away containers.

She looked up.

Malia stood in the doorway.

Kalei looked past her, across the yard.

Then back.

"You walked it."

"Yeah."

"And?"

Malia turned.

From here, the main house sat at the other end of the diagonal.

Nothing blocked the line.

Not one thing.

Every reason existed.

Every movement had a person.

Every person had a reason.

Malia put one hand on the service-door frame.

"It still works."

Kalei looked at the yard.

"Of course it works."

Malia nodded.

That was the problem.
