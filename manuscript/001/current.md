# The Hook

Malia knew the house had been left open before she reached the gate.

The front shutters were folded back against the stone. The kitchen door stood wide enough for steam to leave in visible bursts. Somebody had stretched two lengths of washed cloth between the cistern posts, which meant Kalei had already run out of indoor drying room.

None of that was remarkable.

That was what bothered Malia about noticing it.

She had landed in Hilo less than ninety minutes ago, had been dropped at the Kealoha place with one travel bag cutting into her shoulder, and already her mind was doing inventory.

Main house open.

Kitchen active.

Gear house occupied. Hammering, then silence, then a short cough from an engine that failed to catch.

Storm shutters secured clear of the hinges.

No loose stools in the yard.

Cistern cover seated properly.

She stopped outside the gate and looked at the compound she had spent twelve years insisting she barely remembered.

The gate dragged at the bottom exactly where it always had.

Malia lifted instead of pushing.

The wood cleared the stone.

"Still does that," Koa said.

He was sitting on the low wall beside the gate with one sandal off and a strip of black rubber in his lap. Sixteen now. Long enough in the legs that the memory of a small boy with a bowl haircut had become useless.

Malia let the gate settle behind her. "You could fix it."

"I could."

"Compelling defense."

"I'm fixing this."

He held up the sandal.

The strap had torn through one side. He had threaded a thin piece of cord through the sole and was trying to melt the frayed end with a pocket lighter.

Malia shifted her bag higher. "That's going to last six minutes."

"Seven if I don't run."

He went back to the sandal.

Good. Current problem. Current solution. Nothing to do with Lehua.

Malia crossed the yard.

The family place made more sense from inside the gate.

The main house sat uphill, broad and low, old wood siding silvered by rain beneath a corrugated roof. The detached cookhouse stood to the southeast, newer than the main house but built on an older concrete pad. West of the yard, the workshop had both doors open, and Keoni was half inside an engine casing with his shoulders braced against the frame.

He saw Malia and raised one greasy hand.

"You're early."

"The ferry was early."

"No, it wasn't."

Malia glanced back toward the village path.

Keoni smiled without leaving the engine. "You still move like Waikīkī is timing you."

"I live twenty minutes from my hotel."

"Exactly."

The machine between his knees looked as if several smaller machines had died to keep it alive.

"What is that?"

"Generator off Kimo's boat."

"Should it be making that sound?"

"No."

"Useful."

"That's why he brought it."

He bent back over the casing.

There was something reassuring about being dismissed for an engine.

Malia reached the main house before anyone tried to hug her.

Inside, the air held old stone, clean cloth, ginger, and the faint medicinal smell that had lived in Lehua's room for the last two years.

The front key hung on the iron hook beside the door.

Malia noticed it before she noticed her mother.

Of course she did.

The key was larger than it needed to be, dark iron with a smooth oval bow and a narrow leather loop threaded through it. The hook had been hammered into the wall at shoulder height. Key hanging straight. Leather loop folded behind rather than twisted forward.

Normal.

Nalani sat at the long table with three stacks of paper and a cup that did not belong to her.

She looked up.

"There she is."

Malia dropped her bag.

Then her mother was around the table and hugging her hard enough to make Malia's right arm useless.

Nalani smelled like ferry wind and the citrus soap she bought in bulk because she hated choosing soap.

"You got thinner," Nalani said into her shoulder.

"I absolutely did not."

"Good. I was testing whether grief had made me dishonest."

Malia laughed once despite herself.

Nalani let go and immediately bent for the travel bag.

Malia caught the handle first.

"I have it."

"I can see that. You've had it all morning."

"That's usually how luggage works."

"Your room's the back north."

Malia looked toward the two rear rooms.

"Same one?"

"No. Kalei moved the blue chest into that one, and I don't feel like hearing why it's apparently impossible to move it back."

From the kitchen yard, Kalei shouted, "Because the floor dips there."

Nalani closed her eyes.

Malia picked up her bag.

"Hello, Auntie."

"Wash your hands."

"Hello to you too."

"You walked from the ferry."

That was apparently the end of the argument.

Malia carried her bag to the north room.

The bed had fresh linen. Her mother had set her small case on the floor but had not opened it. A folded blanket sat at the foot, edges aligned with the mattress but not centered. Someone had put a water cup on the sill.

Malia moved the cup away from the open shutter.

A gust could knock it.

That wasn't compulsion. That was gravity.

She left her bag zipped.

In the yard, Koa had successfully reattached the sandal and was testing it by walking with exaggerated care.

"Seven minutes," Malia called.

"You're negative."

"Professionally."

He pointed toward the kitchen. "Auntie says wash."

"I heard."

The kitchen house was hotter than the yard and twice as crowded.

Kalei stood at the worktable with a repair basket open beside her, stitching the corner of a serving cloth while a pot simmered behind her. Two covered trays waited near the door. A neighbor Malia recognized but could not name was slicing fruit. Another woman was rinsing cups.

The days after Tūtū Lehua died.

Not stillness. Work.

People came. They brought food, took food, carried stories in with the containers, and left dishes behind.

Lehua was dead, and the kitchen needed more water.

Malia washed her hands at the basin.

Kalei did not stand up.

"Let me look at you."

Malia turned.

Kalei squinted as if assessing a hem.

"You look tired."

"Mother said thin."

"Your mother lies when she has no useful information."

From the main house, Nalani shouted, "I heard that."

"Good."

Kalei tied off the repair and bit the thread.

Her hair was shorter than Malia remembered. More gray. Her face looked both older and almost offensively familiar.

Malia wanted to hug her and did not know if doing it while Kalei held a needle would be practical.

Kalei solved the problem by reaching out first.

One arm. Quick.

Then she handed Malia the repaired cloth.

"Put that under the yellow tray."

Malia did.

No ceremony.

Better.

The yellow tray was warm underneath from whatever had been placed on it before. Malia lifted it, shook the repaired cloth flat, and set the tray down again.

The cloth corner disappeared under the wood.

She adjusted it so the repaired edge remained visible.

Kalei watched her.

"What?"

"Nothing."

That answer had belonged to Lehua too.

Malia left it alone.

For the next hour she worked.

It was easier than sitting in the main house while people said things about the dead.

She carried water from the cistern.

She moved clean cups away from the rinse basin because somebody had started putting wet ones among them.

She found three serving spoons buried under a cloth and returned them to the jar beside the hearth.

She opened the western kitchen shutter another handspan when smoke began collecting near the ceiling.

Every task had an answer.

Wet cup. Dry shelf.

Full basin. Empty basin.

Food going out. Containers coming back.

At work, Malia trained new supervisors to look for exactly this.

A room never told you what happened in words. It told you through residue.

A towel used and rehung.

A cup with water still under the base.

A drawer closed but not seated.

A bed remade by somebody who did not know how the corners had been folded the first time.

People thought housekeeping was cleaning.

Cleaning was maybe a third of it.

The rest was returning hundreds of small things to an agreed condition before the next person arrived.

By midday, Malia had forgotten to be sad for nearly eleven consecutive minutes.

Then a woman at the door squeezed her arm and said, "You have Lehua's hands."

Malia looked down.

She was holding six cups by their handles, three in each hand.

"Do I?"

"Oh, yes."

The woman smiled as if this were a gift.

Malia set the cups down.

Across the room, Kalei did not look up.

Later, Nalani cornered Malia between the main house table and a stack of empty food tins.

"I want to start the inventory tomorrow."

Malia glanced toward the east room.

Lehua's door was closed.

"We can count without distributing."

"That's what I said."

"I know what you said."

Nalani flattened one of her paper stacks with both palms.

"We have three days before the ferry I want."

"You have three days."

"You don't?"

Malia looked at the papers.

Property records. Repair notes. A list of names she did not recognize.

"I told work I'd be back next week."

"So did I."

"Then we're both leaving."

Nalani picked up her cup.

It was Kalei's brown one, Malia realized. The brown cup had sat on the second shelf of the kitchen for as long as she could remember.

Or maybe it had belonged somewhere else twenty-four years ago.

The thought irritated her.

There was no reason to start sorting childhood shelves in her head.

Nalani drank.

"Kalei will turn this into a six-month ritual if nobody puts dates on anything."

"I can hear you," Kalei said from outside.

"You can hear through stone now?"

"No. You're loud."

Nalani looked at Malia.

"See?"

Malia did not smile.

Her mother did anyway.

Keoni appeared at the open door with both forearms black to the elbow.

"I need the west key."

Kalei answered from the yard. "On the small hook."

Keoni reached past the main key, took a shorter brass key from a second hook lower down, and tucked it into his pocket.

Malia watched without meaning to.

Keoni caught her.

"Checking me in?"

"What?"

"You did the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You've had that face since you were nine."

He wiped one wrist with a rag.

"Kimo needs the generator before dark. If I don't bring it now, he loses the cold box."

"Go."

"Wasn't asking."

He looked toward the yard.

"Koa."

No answer.

Keoni stepped outside.

"Koa!"

From somewhere beyond the wall came, "I heard you."

"Then respond like a mammal."

Malia watched Keoni disappear toward the gear house.

Nalani tapped the papers.

"Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow."

"And Lehua's room."

Malia glanced at the closed door again.

"After Kalei is ready."

Nalani's mouth tightened.

"It's a room."

"It was also where she slept."

"She isn't sleeping."

There it was.

Not cruelty exactly.

Nalani was very good at reducing a thing until she could carry it.

Malia picked up the nearest stack of papers and squared the edges.

Her mother watched her hands.

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Make it neat so you don't have to answer me."

Malia stopped.

Nalani took the stack away and put it down crooked.

Then she left for the kitchen.

Malia stared at the papers.

The top sheet had shifted half an inch left.

She let it.

For almost a minute.

Then Koa came in through the front door, kicked off his repaired sandal, and dumped two folded chairs against the wall.

One slid.

Malia caught it before the wood hit stone.

"Where did those come from?"

"Aunty Nani's."

"Why?"

"She borrowed ours for her daughter's naming last week."

"So these are ours."

"Those are hers."

Malia looked at the chairs.

"They're different."

"Yeah."

"Then why are they here?"

"Because she wants ours tomorrow for the visitors, so I brought hers here now so we still have enough chairs."

Malia waited.

Koa waited back.

"That is a terrible system."

"It works."

"Until everyone owns everyone else's chairs."

"That's basically this village."

He picked up his sandal.

The repair had already started pulling loose.

Malia looked at it.

Koa looked at her.

"Eight minutes."

"You were sitting for most of them."

"Still counts."

He left through the door leading to the yard, one sandal in hand.

Malia moved the borrowed chairs away from the wall so their backs would not scrape the stone.

Then she stopped.

They were not her chairs.

She moved them back.

One inch away from the wall.

A reasonable compromise.

By late afternoon, the place had changed shape three times without anything unusual happening.

Visitors arrived and left.

Food moved from kitchen to main house, then empty containers moved back.

Keoni carried the repaired generator through the west opening with Koa behind him complaining about the weight even though he was carrying only a tool case.

Kalei closed the eastern kitchen shutter when the wind shifted.

Nalani filled one rubbish basket, then another, mostly with paper she had decided nobody needed.

Malia took two things out of the second basket after checking.

One was a receipt from a roof repair six years earlier.

The other was an envelope still containing cash.

Nalani stared at it.

"Fine."

Malia held up the envelope.

"Fine?"

"Useful skill. Horrible personality."

"You raised me."

"Lehua trained you."

The words landed harder than Nalani seemed to intend.

Malia put the envelope on the table.

Neither of them said anything else.

At sunset, the last visitors left.

The road beyond the gate quieted by degrees.

Kalei and Koa carried dishes to the kitchen house.

Keoni had not returned from the harbor.

Nalani took her papers into the rear room and shut the door.

Malia stood in the yard with a stack of bowls against her hip.

The wind had cooled.

From the kitchen came water, ceramic, Kalei telling Koa that if he stacked another wet plate under a dry one she would make him wash every dish again.

"That's not logical," Koa said.

"It will teach you."

"Teach me what?"

"Fear."

Malia laughed.

She carried the bowls in.

They finished without talking much.

Work got quieter when everyone was tired enough.

Kalei put the remaining food away.

Koa checked the catchment tank cover because she told him to.

Malia wiped the table.

She found herself smoothing the cloth along the grain in two long strokes, then one short one at the far edge.

She stopped.

That was not her work pattern.

At the hotel she worked corners first.

She looked at the cloth.

Kalei had already gone outside.

Malia did the last short stroke anyway.

Night gathered fast once the shutters closed.

They crossed back to the main house carrying a lamp and the final clean cups.

Koa used the side path toward the west opening to see whether Keoni had come back.

Kalei checked the main gate, then crossed to the west service opening and dropped the wooden bar into its wall brackets.

Malia checked the main door.

The iron key was still on its hook.

She took it down.

Heavy.

Familiar in a way that bypassed memory.

She locked the door.

One turn. Pull to test. Another small lift of the handle so the old bolt seated cleanly.

Then she hung the key back on the hook.

The leather loop twisted forward.

Malia flattened it behind the key with her thumb.

The key still leaned slightly right.

She nudged the bow left until the iron rested straight against the stone.

Her hand remained there.

Thumb on the leather.

Forefinger on the cool edge of the bow.

She knew that movement.

Not from the hotel.

Not from adulthood.

Lehua had done it every night.

Key straight.

Loop behind.

Nothing caught where it should not.

Malia let go.

The key stayed exactly where she had put it.

Of course it did.
