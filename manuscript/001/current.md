# The Hook

Mara knew the house had been left open before she reached the gate.

The front shutters were folded back against the stone. The kitchen door stood wide enough for steam to leave in visible bursts. Somebody had stretched two lengths of washed cloth between the cistern posts, which meant Inez had already run out of indoor drying room.

None of that was remarkable.

That was what bothered Mara about noticing it.

She had been off the ferry less than forty minutes, had walked up from Salen with one travel bag cutting into her shoulder, and already her mind was doing inventory.

Main house open.

Kitchen active.

Gear house occupied. Hammering, then silence, then a short cough from an engine that failed to catch.

Storm shutters secured clear of the hinges.

No loose stools in the yard.

Cistern cover seated properly.

She stopped outside the gate and looked at the compound she had spent twelve years insisting she barely remembered.

The gate dragged at the bottom exactly where it always had.

Mara lifted instead of pushing.

The wood cleared the stone.

"Still does that," Lio said.

He was sitting on the low wall beside the gate with one sandal off and a strip of black rubber in his lap. Sixteen now. Long enough in the legs that the memory of a small boy with a bowl haircut had become useless.

Mara let the gate settle behind her. "You could fix it."

"I could."

"Compelling defense."

"I'm fixing this."

He held up the sandal.

The strap had torn through one side. He had threaded a thin piece of cord through the sole and was trying to melt the frayed end with a pocket lighter.

Mara shifted her bag higher. "That's going to last six minutes."

"Seven if I don't run."

He went back to the sandal.

Good. Current problem. Current solution. Nothing to do with Adela.

Mara crossed the yard.

The compound made more sense from inside it.

The main house sat north, broad and low, stone walls darkened by years of salt and rain. The kitchen house stood apart to the southeast, newer masonry under an older roofline. West of the yard, the gear house had both doors open, and Tomas was half inside an engine casing with his shoulders braced against the frame.

He saw Mara and raised one greasy hand.

"You're early."

"The ferry was early."

"No, it wasn't."

Mara glanced back toward the village path.

Tomas smiled without leaving the engine. "You're just still on city time."

"I live twenty minutes from my hotel."

"Exactly."

The machine between his knees looked as if several smaller machines had died to keep it alive.

"What is that?"

"Generator off Paren's boat."

"Should it be making that sound?"

"No."

"Useful."

"That's why he brought it."

He bent back over the casing.

There was something reassuring about being dismissed for an engine.

Mara reached the main house before anyone tried to hug her.

Inside, the air held old stone, clean cloth, ginger, and the faint medicinal smell that had lived in Adela's room for the last two years.

The front key hung on the iron hook beside the door.

Mara noticed it before she noticed her mother.

Of course she did.

The key was larger than it needed to be, dark iron with a smooth oval bow and a narrow leather loop threaded through it. The hook had been hammered into the wall at shoulder height. Key hanging straight. Leather loop folded behind rather than twisted forward.

Normal.

Celina sat at the long table with three stacks of paper and a cup that did not belong to her.

She looked up.

"There she is."

Mara dropped her bag.

Then her mother was around the table and hugging her hard enough to make Mara's right arm useless.

Celina smelled like ferry wind and the citrus soap she bought in bulk because she hated choosing soap.

"You got thinner," Celina said into her shoulder.

"I absolutely did not."

"Good. I was testing whether grief had made me dishonest."

Mara laughed once despite herself.

Celina let go and immediately bent for the travel bag.

Mara caught the handle first.

"I have it."

"I can see that. You've had it all morning."

"That's usually how luggage works."

"Your room's the back north."

Mara looked toward the two rear rooms.

"Same one?"

"No. Inez moved the blue chest into that one, and I don't feel like hearing why it's apparently impossible to move it back."

From the kitchen yard, Inez shouted, "Because the floor dips there."

Celina closed her eyes.

Mara picked up her bag.

"Hello, Auntie."

"Wash your hands."

"Hello to you too."

"You walked from the ferry."

That was apparently the end of the argument.

Mara carried her bag to the north room.

The bed had fresh linen. Her mother had set her small case on the floor but had not opened it. A folded blanket sat at the foot, edges aligned with the mattress but not centered. Someone had put a water cup on the sill.

Mara moved the cup away from the open shutter.

A gust could knock it.

That wasn't compulsion. That was gravity.

She left her bag zipped.

In the yard, Lio had successfully reattached the sandal and was testing it by walking with exaggerated care.

"Seven minutes," Mara called.

"You're negative."

"Professionally."

He pointed toward the kitchen. "Auntie says wash."

"I heard."

The kitchen house was hotter than the yard and twice as crowded.

Inez stood at the worktable with a repair basket open beside her, stitching the corner of a serving cloth while a pot simmered behind her. Two covered trays waited near the door. A neighbor Mara recognized but could not name was slicing fruit. Another woman was rinsing cups.

Keeping Days.

Not stillness. Work.

People came. They brought food, took food, carried stories in with the containers, and left dishes behind.

Adela was dead, and the kitchen needed more water.

Mara washed her hands at the basin.

Inez did not stand up.

"Let me look at you."

Mara turned.

Inez squinted as if assessing a hem.

"You look tired."

"Mother said thin."

"Your mother lies when she has no useful information."

From the main house, Celina shouted, "I heard that."

"Good."

Inez tied off the repair and bit the thread.

Her hair was shorter than Mara remembered. More gray. Her face looked both older and almost offensively familiar.

Mara wanted to hug her and did not know if doing it while Inez held a needle would be practical.

Inez solved the problem by reaching out first.

One arm. Quick.

Then she handed Mara the repaired cloth.

"Put that under the yellow tray."

Mara did.

No ceremony.

Better.

The yellow tray was warm underneath from whatever had been placed on it before. Mara lifted it, shook the repaired cloth flat, and set the tray down again.

The cloth corner disappeared under the wood.

She adjusted it so the repaired edge remained visible.

Inez watched her.

"What?"

"Nothing."

That answer had belonged to Adela too.

Mara left it alone.

For the next hour she worked.

It was easier than sitting in the main house while people said things about the dead.

She carried water from the cistern.

She moved clean cups away from the rinse basin because somebody had started putting wet ones among them.

She found three serving spoons buried under a cloth and returned them to the jar beside the hearth.

She opened the western kitchen shutter another handspan when smoke began collecting near the ceiling.

Every task had an answer.

Wet cup. Dry shelf.

Full basin. Empty basin.

Food going out. Containers coming back.

At work, Mara trained new supervisors to look for exactly this.

A room never told you what happened in words. It told you through residue.

A towel used and rehung.

A cup with water still under the base.

A drawer closed but not seated.

A bed remade by somebody who did not know how the corners had been folded the first time.

People thought housekeeping was cleaning.

Cleaning was maybe a third of it.

The rest was returning hundreds of small things to an agreed condition before the next person arrived.

By midday, Mara had forgotten to be sad for nearly eleven consecutive minutes.

Then a woman at the door squeezed her arm and said, "You have Adela's hands."

Mara looked down.

She was holding six cups by their handles, three in each hand.

"Do I?"

"Oh, yes."

The woman smiled as if this were a gift.

Mara set the cups down.

Across the room, Inez did not look up.

Later, Celina cornered Mara between the main house table and a stack of empty food tins.

"I want to start the inventory tomorrow."

Mara glanced toward the east room.

Adela's door was closed.

"We can count without distributing."

"That's what I said."

"I know what you said."

Celina flattened one of her paper stacks with both palms.

"We have three days before the ferry I want."

"You have three days."

"You don't?"

Mara looked at the papers.

Property records. Repair notes. A list of names she did not recognize.

"I told work I'd be back next week."

"So did I."

"Then we're both leaving."

Celina picked up her cup.

It was Inez's brown one, Mara realized. The brown cup had sat on the second shelf of the kitchen for as long as she could remember.

Or maybe it had belonged somewhere else twenty-four years ago.

The thought irritated her.

There was no reason to start sorting childhood shelves in her head.

Celina drank.

"Inez will turn this into a six-month ritual if nobody puts dates on anything."

"I can hear you," Inez said from outside.

"You can hear through stone now?"

"No. You're loud."

Celina looked at Mara.

"See?"

Mara did not smile.

Her mother did anyway.

Tomas appeared at the open door with both forearms black to the elbow.

"I need the west key."

Inez answered from the yard. "On the small hook."

Tomas reached past the main key, took a shorter brass key from a second hook lower down, and tucked it into his pocket.

Mara watched without meaning to.

Tomas caught her.

"Checking me in?"

"What?"

"You did the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You've had that face since you were nine."

He wiped one wrist with a rag.

"Paren needs the generator before dark. If I don't bring it now, he loses the cold box."

"Go."

"Wasn't asking."

He looked toward the yard.

"Lio."

No answer.

Tomas stepped outside.

"Lio!"

From somewhere beyond the wall came, "I heard you."

"Then respond like a mammal."

Mara watched Tomas disappear toward the gear house.

Celina tapped the papers.

"Tomorrow."

"Tomorrow."

"And Adela's room."

Mara glanced at the closed door again.

"After Inez is ready."

Celina's mouth tightened.

"It's a room."

"It was also where she slept."

"She isn't sleeping."

There it was.

Not cruelty exactly.

Celina was very good at reducing a thing until she could carry it.

Mara picked up the nearest stack of papers and squared the edges.

Her mother watched her hands.

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Make it neat so you don't have to answer me."

Mara stopped.

Celina took the stack away and put it down crooked.

Then she left for the kitchen.

Mara stared at the papers.

The top sheet had shifted half an inch left.

She let it.

For almost a minute.

Then Lio came in through the front door, kicked off his repaired sandal, and dumped two folded chairs against the wall.

One slid.

Mara caught it before the wood hit stone.

"Where did those come from?"

"Mrs. Aren's."

"Why?"

"She borrowed ours for her daughter's naming last week."

"So these are ours."

"Those are hers."

Mara looked at the chairs.

"They're different."

"Yeah."

"Then why are they here?"

"Because she wants ours tomorrow for the visitors, so I brought hers here now so we still have enough chairs."

Mara waited.

Lio waited back.

"That is a terrible system."

"It works."

"Until everyone owns everyone else's chairs."

"That's basically this village."

He picked up his sandal.

The repair had already started pulling loose.

Mara looked at it.

Lio looked at her.

"Eight minutes."

"You were sitting for most of them."

"Still counts."

He left through the door leading to the yard, one sandal in hand.

Mara moved the borrowed chairs away from the wall so their backs would not scrape the stone.

Then she stopped.

They were not her chairs.

She moved them back.

One inch away from the wall.

A reasonable compromise.

By late afternoon, the compound had changed shape three times without anything unusual happening.

Visitors arrived and left.

Food moved from kitchen to main house, then empty containers moved back.

Tomas carried the repaired generator through the west opening with Lio behind him complaining about the weight even though he was carrying only a tool case.

Inez closed the eastern kitchen shutter when the wind shifted.

Celina filled one rubbish basket, then another, mostly with paper she had decided nobody needed.

Mara took two things out of the second basket after checking.

One was a receipt from a roof repair six years earlier.

The other was an envelope still containing cash.

Celina stared at it.

"Fine."

Mara held up the envelope.

"Fine?"

"Useful skill. Horrible personality."

"You raised me."

"Adela trained you."

The words landed harder than Celina seemed to intend.

Mara put the envelope on the table.

Neither of them said anything else.

At sunset, the last visitors left.

The village path beyond the gate quieted by degrees.

Inez and Lio carried dishes to the kitchen house.

Tomas had not returned from the harbor.

Celina took her papers into the rear room and shut the door.

Mara stood in the yard with a stack of bowls against her hip.

The wind had cooled.

From the kitchen came water, ceramic, Inez telling Lio that if he stacked another wet plate under a dry one she would make him wash every dish again.

"That's not logical," Lio said.

"It will teach you."

"Teach me what?"

"Fear."

Mara laughed.

She carried the bowls in.

They finished without talking much.

Work got quieter when everyone was tired enough.

Inez put the remaining food away.

Lio checked the cistern lid because she told him to.

Mara wiped the table.

She found herself smoothing the cloth along the grain in two long strokes, then one short one at the far edge.

She stopped.

That was not her work pattern.

At the hotel she worked corners first.

She looked at the cloth.

Inez had already gone outside.

Mara did the last short stroke anyway.

Night gathered fast once the shutters closed.

They crossed back to the main house carrying a lamp and the final clean cups.

Lio used the side path toward the west opening to see whether Tomas had come back.

Inez checked the main gate, then crossed to the west service opening and dropped the wooden bar into its wall brackets.

Mara checked the main door.

The iron key was still on its hook.

She took it down.

Heavy.

Familiar in a way that bypassed memory.

She locked the door.

One turn. Pull to test. Another small lift of the handle so the old bolt seated cleanly.

Then she hung the key back on the hook.

The leather loop twisted forward.

Mara flattened it behind the key with her thumb.

The key still leaned slightly right.

She nudged the bow left until the iron rested straight against the stone.

Her hand remained there.

Thumb on the leather.

Forefinger on the cool edge of the bow.

She knew that movement.

Not from the hotel.

Not from adulthood.

Adela had done it every night.

Key straight.

Loop behind.

Nothing caught where it should not.

Mara let go.

The key stayed exactly where she had put it.

Of course it did.
