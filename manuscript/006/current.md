# Back on the Hook

By the sixth afternoon, the front key had become boring again.

That was good.

Malia wanted boring.

The days around Tūtū Lehua's service had thinned enough that the main house no longer had six people passing through every hour. Aunty Nani still appeared without warning, but Aunty Nani seemed to consider warning a mainland affectation.

The front door stayed closed more often.

The key spent longer on its hook.

Straight.

Leather loop behind.

Malia noticed less.

Mostly.

Rain started just after lunch.

Not hard.

Steady enough to change everybody's route.

Koa brought two folding chairs in from the lanai.

Kalei moved a laundry basket away from the open back window.

Nalani gathered her inventory pages from the table before the damp could curl them.

Keoni came through the west side carrying a coil of hose and said, "Catchment filter's clogging."

Malia looked toward the tank.

"Bad?"

"No."

"Then why did you say it like that?"

"Like what?"

"Like a diagnosis."

"It's a filter."

"Exactly."

Keoni kept walking.

Koa followed him outside with a wrench.

Nalani watched them go.

"Does everybody here answer half a question?"

Kalei said, "You ask too many."

"That is not the same thing."

Malia smiled into her cup.

Kalei went to the front door.

She took the key off the hook.

Malia saw it happen.

Not because she was watching the key.

Because Kalei said, "I'm going down to Aunty Nani's. Her porch light is out."

Nalani looked up.

"In the rain?"

"It's a light."

"Lights can wait."

"Aunty Nani cannot see her step."

Kalei slipped on sandals.

Malia said, "You need the key?"

"So I can lock when I leave."

"The house is occupied."

Kalei looked at her.

"Not forever."

Fair.

She put the key in the pocket of her rain jacket.

Malia recorded that.

Not intentionally.

Just fact.

Key with Kalei.

Kalei left through the front door and pulled it shut behind her.

The hook was empty.

Malia went back to the inventory sheet Nalani had pushed toward her.

"Ceiling fan," Nalani said.

"Functional."

"Condition?"

"Functional."

"That's not a condition."

"It is the only condition that matters."

"Rust."

"Cosmetic."

"One blade is bent."

"Still works."

Nalani wrote FAIR.

Malia looked at the word.

"That's rude."

"It's a fan."

"Still."

They spent fifteen minutes arguing about a lamp.

Then twenty minutes not arguing while rain moved harder across the roof.

The place changed under weather.

Open doors closed.

Shoes collected near entrances.

Towels appeared where wet feet would cross the floor.

The workshop stopped sounding like metal and started sounding like rain.

Malia heard Keoni swear once outside.

Then Koa laughed.

Then both of them went quiet again.

Nalani went to the cookhouse to make coffee.

Malia stayed in the main room.

She was alone for maybe three minutes.

She noticed the front key because it was on the hook.

Malia stopped.

The key hung straight.

Leather loop behind.

Exactly normal.

She looked at the door.

Closed.

The room stayed very ordinary around it.

Malia stood.

She walked to the hook.

Did not touch the key.

Kalei had taken it.

Malia had seen her put it in the rain jacket.

Kalei had not come back through the front door.

Malia was sure of that.

Mostly.

She had been at the table.

Then Nalani had been talking about the fan.

Then she had gone to the cookhouse.

Keoni and Koa were outside.

The rain had made the roof loud.

The west access was open for work.

Malia looked toward the back of the house.

There were other doors.

There were other people.

There were a dozen ways to move a key without performing it for her.

She stayed beside the hook anyway.

Nalani came back carrying two cups.

"What?"

Malia turned.

"What?"

"You're doing the face."

"I don't have a face."

"You absolutely have a face."

Nalani handed her a cup.

Malia took it.

"Did Kalei come back?"

Nalani looked toward the front door.

"Not that I saw."

"Did you see this?"

Malia pointed at the key.

Nalani looked.

"The key?"

"Yeah."

"What about it?"

"Kalei took it."

"Okay."

"It's back."

Nalani drank coffee.

"Okay."

Malia stared at her.

"That doesn't bother you?"

"Should it?"

"She hasn't come back."

"Maybe she did."

"I was here."

"You were inventorying a ceiling fan."

"I would have heard."

Nalani raised one eyebrow.

"Rain."

Malia looked toward the roof.

The rain hit hard enough now that the gutters rattled.

"Did Koa come through?"

"Probably."

"Probably?"

"He moves around."

"Keoni?"

"I don't track him."

That word.

Track.

Malia looked back at the key.

Nalani saw something in her face.

"No."

"What?"

"Don't start doing detective work over a key."

"I'm not."

"You asked who moved through the house."

"Because the key moved."

"It's a key. That's its job."

"Keys unlock things."

"They also get carried."

"Exactly."

Nalani smiled without humor.

"You're proving my point for me."

Malia put the coffee down.

She still did not touch the key.

"If Kalei took it to lock the front door when she left, then it shouldn't be here unless someone brought it back."

"Correct."

"Who?"

Nalani shrugged.

"Someone."

"That's not an answer."

"It is if the question doesn't matter."

Malia looked at her.

Nalani pointed at the hook.

"You want one person responsible for every object because then the room behaves."

"That is not what I'm doing."

"It is exactly what you're doing."

Malia hated how easily the sentence landed.

Nalani walked back toward the cookhouse.

"Ask Kalei when she comes back."

Simple.

Malia stood alone again.

The key hung there.

Normal.

That was the problem.

If it had been on the table, she could have called it ordinary movement.

If it had been in the door, ordinary use.

If it had been in Keoni's pocket, current need.

But it was back in the exact state it had occupied before Kalei took it.

Straight.

Loop behind.

Restored.

Malia told herself not to be stupid.

Then Koa came in through the back.

Wet hair.

No shoes.

He went straight to the towel by the door.

Malia said, "Did you put the front key back?"

Koa looked at her.

"No."

"Did you see Kalei?"

"No."

"Did your dad?"

"I don't know."

"Did either of you come through the front?"

"No."

Malia waited.

Koa dried his hair.

"Why?"

"Nothing."

He lowered the towel.

"That's suspicious."

"Because I said nothing?"

"Because that's the family word."

Malia sighed.

"The key is back."

Koa glanced over.

"So?"

"Kalei took it."

"Okay."

Malia stared at him.

"You are all impossible."

"No, you're just asking everybody to remember boring things."

"That is literally how this house works."

Koa tossed the towel over his shoulder.

"No. That's how you work."

He went toward his room.

Malia stood very still.

Then she went outside.

Rain under the lanai edge hit the concrete in a continuous sheet.

Keoni was kneeling beside a plastic housing connected to the catchment line.

He had the filter open.

Koa had left the wrench beside him.

"Did you touch the front key?"

Keoni looked up.

"No."

"Did Kalei give it to you?"

"No."

"Did you see her?"

"No."

Malia crossed her arms.

Keoni looked back at the filter.

"You done?"

"Why is everyone annoyed?"

"Because you're asking the same question until somebody gives you an answer you like."

"I don't have an answer I like."

"Then good."

He pulled a screen free.

Leaf fragments and roof debris clung to it.

Malia watched him rinse it with collected water from a bucket.

"How often do you clean that?"

"When it needs it."

"Useful."

"You keep asking questions with variable answers and getting mad they vary."

Malia looked at him.

"That's profound for a filter."

"It's not profound."

He put the screen back.

"House has a lot of hands in it."

"Family place."

Keoni glanced up.

"What?"

"Nothing."

He tightened the housing.

Malia almost smiled.

Then Kalei came through the gate.

No umbrella.

Rain jacket dark with water.

Empty hands.

Malia watched her come across the yard.

Kalei saw her.

"What?"

"Did you bring the front key back?"

Kalei stopped.

"The key?"

"Yeah."

Kalei patted the rain-jacket pocket.

Empty.

Then the other.

She frowned.

"I thought I had it."

Malia looked toward the main house.

"It's on the hook."

Kalei followed her gaze through the open back doorway.

"Huh."

Malia waited.

Kalei unzipped the jacket.

Checked both pockets again.

Nothing.

"Did you give it to somebody?"

"Maybe."

Malia felt irritation rise.

"Maybe?"

"I stopped at Nani's, then Lani's boy came by because he needed the extension cord from the workshop."

"Which boy?"

"Micah."

"Did you give Micah the key?"

"No."

"Then who?"

Kalei thought.

"Maybe I gave it to Koa before I left."

"Koa says no."

"Then I didn't."

Malia stared.

Kalei looked at her.

"What?"

"You don't remember?"

"I was leaving in rain to fix a porch light."

"It's the front key."

"Yes."

"How do you not remember who you gave it to?"

Kalei's face changed.

Not angry.

Tired.

"Because I don't make a record every time I hand someone a key."

Malia stopped.

There it was.

Too close.

Kalei took off the rain jacket.

"Was the door locked?"

"No."

"Was anything missing?"

"No."

"Did somebody who shouldn't have it get inside?"

"Not that I know."

"Then what is the problem?"

Malia opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Kalei hung the jacket on a peg.

"Ask better questions."

Then she went to change.

Malia stayed under the lanai.

Keoni looked up from the filter.

He did not say anything.

That was worse.

Malia went back inside.

The key remained on the hook.

She took it down.

Heavy.

Cool.

Ordinary.

She examined the leather loop.

Nothing.

No water.

No grit.

No clue.

Of course not.

A key did not preserve its chain of custody out of politeness.

Nalani came in behind her.

"You asked Kalei."

"Yes."

"And?"

"She doesn't know."

Nalani set down a dish towel.

"Then you have your answer."

"No, I have less answer."

"Same thing sometimes."

Malia put the key back.

Straight.

Loop behind.

Her hand paused.

Then she stopped.

No.

She took it down again.

Nalani watched.

Malia turned the leather loop forward.

Let the key lean slightly right against the wall.

Not dramatic.

Barely wrong.

A position she would have corrected automatically two days ago.

Nalani said, "What are you doing?"

Malia looked at her.

"Nothing."

Nalani laughed once.

"You don't get to use that word."

"Apparently I do."

Nalani looked at the key.

Then at Malia.

"You testing something?"

Malia's pulse changed.

Just enough.

"No."

The lie arrived too quickly.

Nalani saw that too.

Malia took her coffee cup to the kitchen before her mother could answer.

She told herself the test was harmless.

Nobody would know.

Nobody needed to know.

If the key stayed like that, fine.

If somebody used it, fine.

If somebody corrected it, also fine.

She would only observe.

That was all.

Observe.

Not accuse.

Not control.

Not interfere.

Just see what the house did when nobody knew they were being watched.

The thought should have bothered her more than it did.
