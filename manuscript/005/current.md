# Tūtū Lehua's Room

Nalani brought the inventory sheets to breakfast.

Kalei looked at them.

Then she kept eating.

That was the entire opening argument.

Malia sat between them with toast in one hand and the green cracked cup in front of her because every other everyday cup was already in use.

Koa had the blue one.

Keoni was not there.

Again.

"He left before six," Kalei said without being asked.

Malia looked at her.

"I didn't ask."

"You were going to."

"Where?"

"Boat ramp."

"What broke?"

Koa said, "A boat."

Malia pointed at him with the toast.

"You're becoming unpleasant."

"I'm sixteen."

Nalani slid one page across the table.

"Today."

Kalei drank her coffee.

"After breakfast."

"That is today."

"Then why are you saying it like I disagreed?"

Nalani looked at Malia.

Malia looked at the green crack facing outward.

She had no jurisdiction over sisters before eight in the morning.

The page on the table was headed:

LEHUA KEALOHA
PERSONAL ROOM

Below that, Nalani had drawn four columns.

Item.

Condition.

Keep.

Decide.

Malia read them twice.

"You're missing owner."

Nalani looked at her.

"Owner is the estate."

Kalei set down her cup.

"No."

Nalani's eyes moved to her.

"No?"

"Not everything in that room was hers."

"Then we identify it."

"And not everything that was hers is yours to decide."

"I didn't say mine."

"You wrote 'keep.'"

"I wrote a category."

"Whose category?"

Nalani's mouth tightened.

Malia put down the toast.

There it was.

Not a fight yet.

Just the place where one could happen.

"We can start with what is obviously Lehua's," Malia said.

Nalani turned toward her.

"Thank you."

Kalei said, "And when it isn't obvious?"

"We ask."

"Who?"

Malia paused.

Kalei's expression did not change.

That was the question.

Who knew what Lehua had considered hers?

Who knew what had been borrowed?

Who knew what had been given?

Who knew what had been kept only because nobody had thrown it away?

Who knew what had belonged to Nohea twenty-four years ago and survived because the house was very good at keeping things nobody wanted to discuss?

Malia looked at the page again.

Nalani had made four columns because columns behaved.

People did not.

"One room," Malia said. "We start with one room."

Kalei wiped her fingers on a napkin.

"Fine."

Nalani gathered the papers immediately.

Kalei did not move.

Nalani stopped.

"What?"

"I said after breakfast."

"You finished."

"I haven't."

"You stopped eating."

"I stopped chewing."

Koa bent his head over his plate.

Malia could see his shoulders moving.

"Koa."

He looked up with a completely neutral face.

"Yeah?"

"Go do literally anything else."

"Gladly."

He took his cup and left through the back door.

Kalei watched him go.

"Take the cup back when you're done."

"Yeah."

Malia looked at Nalani.

"Current household authority."

Nalani did not smile.

Kalei did.

Barely.

After breakfast, the three of them stood outside Tūtū Lehua's room.

The door was not locked.

Malia had known that.

Still, some part of her had expected Kalei to produce a key.

Nalani put one hand on the knob.

Kalei said, "Wait."

Nalani froze.

Malia looked at her aunt.

Kalei stepped past them, opened the door herself, and pushed it wide.

No ceremony.

No prayer.

No announcement to the dead.

She walked in and opened both windows.

"Room's been closed too much."

Nalani stayed in the doorway.

"You've been in here."

"Every day."

"Since she died?"

"Yes."

Nalani stared at her.

Kalei crossed to the bed and stripped off the light cotton cover.

"Sheets don't stop needing air because somebody dies."

Nalani looked at Malia.

Malia did not help.

The room was smaller than memory.

That was the first thing.

The second was how little of Tūtū Lehua seemed to be in it.

A narrow bed.

Washstand.

Low dresser.

One chair.

Built-in shelf.

Two framed photographs.

A box fan near the wall.

A stack of folded towels.

The room of an old woman who had spent most of her time elsewhere in the house because life happened elsewhere.

No shrine.

No preserved last moment.

The bed had been remade.

The trash was empty.

The windowsills had been wiped.

A drinking glass sat upside down on a folded cloth.

Kalei carried the bed cover outside to the lanai rail.

Nalani followed her with her eyes.

"You changed the sheets."

"Yes."

"When?"

"Two days ago."

"Why?"

Kalei came back in.

"Because people were coming."

"Nobody was sleeping here."

"Still needed changing."

Nalani looked at the bed.

Malia understood both of them.

That was becoming exhausting.

Nalani saw a dead woman's room being altered before the family had decided what to do with it.

Kalei saw laundry.

Malia walked to the washstand.

On the right side sat a shallow ceramic dish.

Dark blue rim.

White center.

Inside it lay a comb.

Black-brown horn, wide teeth, one tooth near the end worn shorter than the others.

Malia recognized it immediately.

Not emotionally.

Practically.

Tūtū Lehua had used the same comb for as long as Malia could remember.

Malia picked it up.

Kalei said, "Put it back."

Malia did.

Fast enough to annoy herself.

Nalani looked over.

"Why?"

Kalei pointed.

"That's where she kept it."

Malia looked at the dish.

"Always?"

"Last fifteen years, at least."

"What about before?"

Kalei gave her a look.

"You going to inventory history now too?"

Nalani said, "Apparently that's my job."

Kalei ignored her.

Malia studied the comb.

The dish itself had a ring of dust around the edge but almost none underneath the comb.

Someone had lifted it recently.

Kalei saw her looking.

"I cleaned it."

"The comb?"

"The dish."

"When?"

"Yesterday."

Malia looked at her.

Kalei looked back.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"No. Say it."

Malia touched the edge of the washstand.

"Why clean the dish if nobody's using it?"

Kalei's face changed, not much.

"Because there was hair in it."

Nalani turned.

"Whose?"

Kalei stared at her.

"Lehua's."

The answer was so obvious it made Nalani look briefly ashamed.

Kalei continued.

"She lost hair. I cleaned the brush and comb every few days. There was hair in the dish after."

"So you kept doing it."

"One more time."

Nalani looked away.

Malia wished the room had something else to look at.

It did.

Too much, actually.

The low dresser had three drawers.

Top drawer open one inch.

Middle closed.

Bottom swollen enough that the wood did not sit flush.

Malia touched the top drawer.

Kalei said, "Underclothes."

Malia removed her hand.

Nalani said, "We have to inventory clothes."

"Then inventory the clothes."

"You just told her not to open it."

"I told her what was inside."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

Nalani moved toward the dresser.

Kalei did not block her.

That almost made it worse.

Nalani pulled the top drawer open.

Folded underwear.

Nightshirts.

Two bras.

A cloth pouch.

Medication blister packs, empty.

Nalani looked at Kalei.

"Why are the meds in here?"

"Old packs."

"Shouldn't they be thrown away?"

"Probably."

"Then why aren't they?"

"Because I didn't get to it."

Kalei's voice was flat.

Not defensive.

More tired than that.

Nalani picked up one empty blister pack.

"How long were you doing this?"

Kalei looked at her.

"What?"

"Everything."

Malia felt the room narrow.

Kalei took the blister pack from Nalani.

"Long enough."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the one you get."

Nalani's face hardened.

Malia stepped in before the room became about twenty years instead of one drawer.

"Can we separate the caregiving stuff from the estate stuff?"

Kalei said, "You can try."

Nalani said, "That's what I'm trying to do."

"No," Kalei said. "You're trying to make it clean."

The words hit Malia too.

Nalani heard that.

Her eyes flicked toward Malia.

Nobody said anything for a few seconds.

Then Malia crouched at the second drawer.

"Can I?"

Kalei nodded.

Malia opened it.

Handkerchiefs.

Old receipts.

Three small sewing kits.

A plastic envelope with warranty papers for a fan that had probably died years ago.

Two unopened packs of batteries.

A flashlight.

A folded grocery bag.

Nothing sacred.

Nothing dramatic.

Everything intensely specific.

Nalani started writing.

"Three sewing kits."

"Two are mine," Kalei said.

Nalani stopped.

"Which?"

"Blue and yellow."

"Why are they in here?"

"Because she used them."

"So hers."

"No."

Nalani looked up.

Kalei folded her arms.

"They're mine."

"But she used them."

"Yes."

Nalani stared at the page.

Malia watched the category collapse in real time.

Item.

Condition.

Keep.

Decide.

No column for belongs to somebody else but lived here for years.

No column for used by the dead but owned by the living.

No column for impossible to separate because family life had been happening the entire time.

Nalani crossed out something.

Malia said, "Maybe stop writing ownership until we know."

Nalani looked irritated.

Then she drew a fifth column.

ASK.

Malia almost laughed.

Kalei did not.

The third drawer stuck.

Malia pulled.

Nothing.

Kalei came over.

"Lift first."

Malia lifted the drawer face slightly and pulled.

It opened.

"Floor settled years ago," Kalei said.

Inside were towels, old sheets, and two folded pāʻū.

Nalani touched one.

Kalei said, "That one is mine."

Nalani took her hand away.

Again.

Faster this time.

Malia noticed.

They worked for another hour.

Not quickly.

Every object created a tiny jurisdiction problem.

A sweater Nalani remembered buying but Lehua had worn for years.

A framed picture Keoni had made in elementary school.

A small fan Kalei had purchased with her own money.

A plastic hair clip that belonged to nobody anybody could identify.

A jewelry box that was definitely Lehua's but contained two rings Kalei said were not.

A flashlight everyone had used.

A stack of old envelopes with names on them.

Malia kept waiting for something to become important.

Nothing did.

That was the importance.

A life had not sorted itself in anticipation of death.

At one point Nalani opened the bedside drawer.

Kalei said, "Careful."

Nalani stopped.

"What?"

"The back sticks."

Nalani pulled gently.

The drawer opened.

Inside:

reading glasses.

Lip balm.

A folded washcloth.

Nail clippers.

A small bottle of lotion.

Two cough drops fused slightly to their wrappers.

Nalani stared.

Malia stared too.

Kalei reached in and removed the washcloth.

Nalani's hand closed around the inventory sheet.

"Why are you taking that?"

"It's damp."

Malia looked.

It was.

Barely.

"How?"

Kalei held it up.

"I used it this morning."

Nalani said nothing.

Kalei went to the washstand.

She wet the cloth again.

Then wiped the inside edge of the window frame.

Dust came away gray.

Malia watched.

"You use her washcloth to clean?"

"That one, yes."

"Was it hers?"

"Probably."

Nalani laughed once.

Not because it was funny.

"Probably."

Kalei dropped the cloth into the laundry basket.

"It's a washcloth."

Nalani said, "It was in her bedside drawer."

"And now it's dirty."

Malia looked at the open drawer.

Reading glasses.

Lip balm.

Nail clippers.

Lotion.

No washcloth.

A changed state.

Completely explained.

Her mind still recorded it.

That bothered her.

Malia said, "Did she keep it there?"

Kalei looked at her.

"What?"

"The washcloth."

"Sometimes."

"For what?"

"Wiping her glasses. Hands. Face."

"So it had a use."

"Yes."

"And now you use it for cleaning."

"Yes."

Nalani said, "Because she doesn't need it."

Kalei turned.

There was no softness in either woman's face now.

"No," Kalei said. "Because it is a cloth."

Nalani looked away first.

Malia closed the drawer.

Carefully.

Not because of Lehua.

Because the runner stuck.

That distinction mattered.

By noon they had inventoried less than half the room.

Nalani hated that.

Kalei seemed unsurprised.

Malia had developed a headache.

Koa appeared in the doorway.

"You guys eating?"

Nalani said, "In a minute."

Kalei said, "Yes."

Koa looked between them.

"Which one?"

"Yes," Malia said.

He nodded.

"Helpful."

He left.

Kalei followed.

Nalani stayed.

Malia started toward the door.

Her mother said, "You think she's right."

Malia stopped.

"About what?"

"All of it."

"No."

"Some of it."

"Yes."

Nalani looked at the room.

"She acts like because she stayed, everything belongs to her."

Malia looked at the open drawers.

"No. She acts like because she stayed, she knows how everything works."

"Same thing."

"It's not."

Nalani folded the inventory sheet.

"Close enough."

Malia shook her head.

"No."

Her mother looked at her.

Malia pointed toward the dresser.

"Her sewing kits. Her fan. Her cleaning cloth now. She had things in here too."

"Because she was taking care of Mom."

"Tūtū."

Nalani's face tightened.

Malia had not meant the correction as a weapon.

It landed like one anyway.

Nalani looked toward the washstand.

The comb sat in the ceramic dish.

Dark horn.

One worn tooth.

Exactly where it belonged.

"Fine," Nalani said.

Not agreement.

Just an end.

She left.

Malia stayed another minute.

The room was quieter without them.

She walked to the washstand.

Did not touch the comb.

Beside the dish sat a small bottle of hair oil.

Half full.

Malia looked at it.

No idea whose.

She could ask Kalei.

She could ask Nalani.

She could write it on a list.

She could decide that anything inside Lehua's room belonged to Lehua until proven otherwise.

That would be neat.

That would also be wrong.

From outside, Kalei called, "Malia."

"Coming."

Malia stepped toward the door.

Then stopped.

The top dresser drawer was still open.

She almost closed it.

Almost.

Instead she called, "Kalei?"

"What?"

"Do you want the drawer open?"

A pause.

Then Kalei said, "Leave it. I need the medication packs."

Malia looked at the drawer.

Underclothes.

Nightshirts.

Empty blister packs.

Things Tūtū Lehua had worn.

Things Kalei still had to deal with.

"Okay."

She left it open.

That felt less tidy.

It also felt more respectful.
