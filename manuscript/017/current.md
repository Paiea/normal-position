# What Lehua Watched

The green notebook was under the phone book.

Malia had seen it before.

That annoyed her.

For days, the family had been arguing about bills, repairs, ownership, access, memory, ghosts they were not calling ghosts, and what exactly the family place was doing.

The notebook had sat under an old phone book nobody used.

Green vinyl cover.

Bent corners.

One strip of clear tape across the spine.

Kalei pulled it out after breakfast.

"This?"

Nalani looked at it.

"That's still here?"

"You asked where the house records were."

"I asked a week ago."

"You were busy."

"With what?"

Kalei stared.

Nalani waved one hand.

"Fine."

Malia reached for the notebook.

Kalei kept hold of it.

Not possessive.

Automatic.

Malia stopped.

"Can I?"

Kalei looked at her hand.

Then gave it over.

Small improvement.

The cover felt soft from years of use.

Not age exactly.

Handling.

Malia opened it.

First page:

WATER

Under that:

tank clean 2017  
pump replaced 2019  
north gutter brackets 3  
filter housing cracked 2022  
Keoni fixed temp  
replace before heavy rain

Malia looked up.

"Thrilling."

Keoni, eating toast, said, "That pump lasted another year."

Kalei said, "Because you kept saying temporary."

"Temporary can last."

Malia turned the page.

Insurance numbers.

Utility account names.

A list of freezer contents.

Three phone numbers with no names.

A note:

NANI HAS LARGE COOLER

Below it:

RETURN LID

Aunty Nani was not present to defend herself.

Probably safer.

Malia kept reading.

The notebook was not Lehua's diary.

It was worse for that.

Not emotionally worse.

Useful worse.

Actual life.

Roof measurements.

Medication times from one period.

Grocery totals.

Who had a key when one went missing.

Names beside borrowed folding chairs.

A list of people who had brought food after somebody's funeral without identifying whose funeral.

Koa leaned over Malia's shoulder.

"Any secrets?"

"Eight cans tuna."

"Dark."

Nalani said, "Go away."

Koa did.

Malia turned three more pages.

Then stopped.

FRONT DOOR

key hook high  
west key low  
check loop flat  
if loop forward ask who came in

Malia read it twice.

Nalani saw her face.

"What?"

Malia turned the notebook.

Nalani read.

Her expression did not change.

"Yeah."

Kalei looked.

Then back at her coffee.

Keoni said, "What?"

Malia read it aloud.

He stopped chewing.

Koa came back from the hallway.

"Thought I was supposed to go away."

"No one can make you."

"True."

He sat.

Malia tapped the page.

"She wrote it."

Nalani said, "I told you."

"Moving things."

"Yeah."

"This isn't moving. This is checking."

"Same system."

Kalei said, "Sometimes she was checking whether the door had been used."

Nalani looked at her.

"Exactly."

"That's not automatically sinister."

"I didn't say sinister."

"You said it like you meant prison warden."

Nalani leaned back.

"If your mother is recording the angle of a key loop to figure out who came home, that's not normal."

Kalei looked at Malia.

Malia hated being brought in before anybody asked.

"At a hotel, yes."

Nalani stared.

Malia corrected herself.

"At a hotel, tracking access can be normal."

"This isn't a hotel."

"I know."

Keoni said, "What was the reason?"

Malia looked at him.

"What?"

"For the note."

He pointed.

"Could've been after somebody left the door unlocked."

Kalei nodded.

"Probably."

Nalani laughed.

"Probably."

Kalei looked at her.

"You want everything to have been about control."

"And you want everything to have been practical."

"No."

"Yes."

"No."

Malia put one hand on the notebook.

"Can we read before we convict a dead woman or acquit her?"

Both sisters looked at her.

Nalani said, "You sound like her."

That hurt too fast to hide.

Nobody spoke.

Nalani's face changed.

She almost apologized.

Did not.

Malia looked back at the page.

"Okay."

Kalei said quietly, "Keep reading."

Malia did.

Two pages later:

WEST DOOR  
Koa's grandfather forgot latch twice  
tie red cord until repair

Practical.

Then:

MISSING SCISSORS  
not in sewing  
not kitchen  
check towel drawer  
ask girls

Malia laughed before she could stop.

Koa looked over.

"What?"

"Nohea."

She showed him.

Koa read.

"Why towel drawer?"

"Because apparently that's where tools went to die."

Keoni said, "Told you."

Ordinary.

Useful.

Then:

BROWN CUP CHIPPED  
turn crack out  
do not give guests

Malia looked toward the cookhouse.

Current green-cup rule had inherited that logic.

Damage visible.

Do not hand a compromised cup to somebody who would not know.

Practical.

Another page:

RAIN  
move shoes inside before dark  
lantern front  
towels by back  
children ask before yard

Nalani's mouth tightened.

Kalei saw.

"Weather."

"I know what rain is."

"Then don't."

"Don't what?"

"Make everything evidence."

Nalani pointed to the notebook.

"She literally made everything evidence."

Kalei's face hardened.

"No. She made notes."

Malia kept reading.

A list of medications.

Appointments.

Roof patch dates.

What looked like a recipe, except the quantities changed three times.

Then:

MALIA HOME 10:40  
said 10  
sand on step  
key straight  
did not hear gate

Malia stopped breathing for one second.

Koa read over her shoulder.

"Oh."

Malia closed the notebook.

Not slammed.

Closed.

Nobody spoke.

Nalani said, "How old?"

Malia did not answer.

She opened it again.

Looked at nearby dates.

No year.

But a note three pages earlier mentioned school uniforms and Keoni borrowing a truck he definitely had not owned when Malia was ten.

Teenage years.

Maybe seventeen.

Maybe eighteen.

Malia read it again.

MALIA HOME 10:40  
said 10  
sand on step  
key straight  
did not hear gate

Keoni said, "She knew you came in."

"Yes."

"How?"

Malia pointed.

"Sand. Maybe."

"And key."

"Key says someone used it."

"Straight says someone put it back right."

Malia looked at him.

Exactly.

Not proof of guilt.

Proof of contact.

Proof of somebody knowing the rule.

Nalani said, "She waited until morning."

Malia looked at her.

"You remember?"

"Not that night. Other nights."

"What did she do?"

"Asked questions she already knew the answer to."

Malia's stomach tightened.

Kalei said, "Sometimes because you lied."

Nalani turned.

"Children lie."

"Teenagers definitely lie."

"So you monitor them with objects?"

"You check if they came home."

"Those are not the same."

"They overlap."

There it was.

Again.

Care and control occupying the same square inch.

Malia reopened the notebook.

The next note made it worse.

SHOES MOVED  
left pair straight  
right pair crossed  
see who fixes

Malia stared.

Nalani did not need to read it.

She said, "There."

Kalei looked.

Her mouth tightened.

Keoni said, "That's different."

Kalei looked at him.

"How?"

"Door check can be safety. That is a trap."

The word sat there.

Trap.

Malia hated how cleanly it fit.

Kalei said, "Maybe she was trying to know whose shoes they were."

"They were shoes."

"You don't know."

"Neither do you."

Malia looked at the note.

Left pair straight.

Right pair crossed.

See who fixes.

A tiny wrongness created deliberately.

Wait.

Observe.

Learn who notices.

She had done that.

Not similar.

Done that.

Front key.

Leather loop forward.

Key leaning right.

Tell nobody.

See who corrects it.

Her hand came off the notebook.

Nalani noticed.

"What?"

Malia looked at her mother.

Then at Keoni.

Then Kalei.

"I did this."

No one asked what she meant.

They knew.

Koa did not.

He looked around.

"Did what?"

Malia swallowed.

"The key."

Koa frowned.

"Your test?"

"Yeah."

"You moved it wrong."

"Yeah."

"To see who fixed it."

"Yeah."

Koa looked at the notebook.

Then back at her.

"Oh."

That was worse than accusation.

Malia pushed the notebook away.

Keoni caught it before it slid off the table.

He put it flat.

Not back to Malia.

Just safe.

Kalei said, "You stopped."

Malia looked at her.

"After."

"You recognized it."

"After."

Nalani said nothing.

Malia wanted her to.

Then did not.

Koa said, "So Tūtū invented trolling."

Keoni laughed once.

Nobody else did.

Koa looked at them.

"Too soon."

"Very," Malia said.

He stood.

"Okay."

Then paused.

"Does it matter that I fixed the key because it looked wrong?"

Malia looked at him.

"Yes."

"How?"

She had no answer.

Koa waited.

Then shrugged and left.

Malia looked at the notebook again.

Keoni said, "Don't turn this into proof she caused anything."

"I wasn't."

"You were thinking."

"I was thinking she saw things."

"Different."

"Maybe."

"No. Different."

Keoni tapped the closed notebook.

"Person can be controlling and observant without being supernatural."

"I know."

"Good."

Kalei reached for the notebook.

Malia let her take it.

Kalei flipped backward.

Past water records.

Past tax notes.

Past medication lists.

She knew where she was going.

That bothered Nalani immediately.

"You've read this."

"Parts."

"Which parts?"

"House parts."

"Everything is house parts."

Kalei ignored her.

She stopped at an older section.

The handwriting changed slightly.

Same hand.

Less shaky.

Darker ink.

She read silently.

Then turned the notebook around.

WINDOW EAST  
latch sticks  
Malia says closed  
cloth moved  
ask again

Malia read.

Nothing supernatural.

Maybe she had lied.

Maybe the cloth moved from air.

Maybe Lehua had touched it herself.

The note did not say.

Next line:

NEXT DAY CLOTH BACK  
nobody says moved  
leave

Malia's skin tightened.

Nalani said, "What does that mean?"

Kalei shook her head.

"I don't know."

Keoni took the notebook.

Read it.

Then the page before.

Then after.

"Could be cleaning cloth."

"Could be."

"Could be Mom forgot."

"Could be."

Malia looked at the word:

leave

Not investigate.

Not ritual.

Not fear.

Leave.

Another page:

BACK STEP BROOM  
put west wall  
morning east  
Kalei says no  
Keoni too young  
wind?

Keoni stared.

"Too young."

Kalei read.

"What year?"

They found a tax receipt tucked between pages.

Twenty-eight years ago.

Keoni would have been nine.

Malia fourteen.

Nohea already dead by then.

Keoni said, "I could move a broom at nine."

Kalei said, "Apparently she didn't think so."

Nalani looked at the question mark after wind.

"She noticed."

Malia said nothing.

The note was not revelation.

It did not prove Lehua knew about the family place.

It did not prove the broom moved itself.

It proved something more irritating.

Lehua had seen unexplained placement too.

Maybe.

And then done what Malia did.

Listed people.

Checked access.

Looked for residue.

Tried weather.

Human first.

Keoni said, "Don't make her right because we know something happened now."

Malia looked at him.

"What?"

"This."

He pointed to the broom note.

"We have one confirmed impossible return. Twenty-eight-year-old broom note is still a broom note."

"Yes."

"Good."

Nalani said, "But she was watching."

Keoni nodded.

"Definitely."

Kalei closed the notebook.

"She watched because she ran the house."

Nalani laughed.

"No. She ran the house by watching."

Kalei stared.

"Same thing sometimes."

"No."

"Sometimes."

Malia looked between them.

There was the whole problem.

At the hotel, Malia had cameras.

Key logs.

Room status.

Maintenance tickets.

Housekeeping boards.

Lost-and-found chain.

Inventory.

People were paid to participate in the system.

They knew the system existed.

Mostly.

A family did not clock in.

Koa did not consent to being tracked because his wet jacket hung on a peg.

Teenage Malia had not agreed that returning a key neatly could become evidence she lied about curfew.

Nohea had not agreed that the location of a cup could tell Lehua whether she had asked permission to drink.

Malia said, "The difference is whether people know."

Kalei looked at her.

"What?"

"At work."

Malia touched the notebook.

"If I track a master key, everybody knows the master key is tracked."

Nalani nodded slowly.

Malia continued.

"If I check who entered a room, there is a system. Policy. Not me moving something two inches and waiting to see who fixes it."

Kalei said, "Families don't have policies."

"That's my point."

"No, it isn't."

Kalei leaned forward.

"You think knowing makes it clean."

Malia stopped.

Kalei tapped the notebook.

"Mom knew if somebody left the gate open because goats got out."

"We don't have goats."

"Not now."

"Fine."

"She knew if somebody used the good knife on cardboard because the edge changed."

Nalani said, "She knew because she inspected everything."

"Because then the knife still worked when you needed it."

"Or because no one could breathe without her knowing."

"Both."

Kalei's voice sharpened for the first time.

"Why does it have to be one?"

No one answered.

Kalei opened the notebook again.

Found a page.

Read:

FREEZER  
Koa's grandfather left lid loose  
fish soft  
move basket in front until seal fixed

She pointed.

"Control?"

Nalani said, "No."

"She blocked the freezer."

"Because it wasn't sealing."

"Exactly."

Another:

MEDS  
morning left tray  
night right  
if still right at breakfast ask

Kalei looked at Malia.

"Control?"

Malia read again.

Medication tracking.

A physical system designed so the position itself told the caregiver whether a dose had likely been taken.

"Care," Malia said.

Nalani's jaw tightened.

Kalei looked at her.

Nalani said, "That doesn't make the shoe thing care."

"No."

Finally.

A clean no.

Kalei sat back.

"It doesn't."

The room loosened by one degree.

Malia looked at the notebook.

That was worse in a useful way.

If every system had been controlling, they could reject all of it.

If every system had been care, they could forgive all of it.

Instead:

medication tray.

Useful.

Freezer basket.

Useful.

Key orientation.

Maybe safety.

Curfew surveillance.

Not clean.

Shoes deliberately crossed.

Trap.

Same eye.

Different use.

Malia understood that too well.

Her job trained her to notice.

What she did with noticing was the moral part.

That sentence arrived almost fully formed.

She distrusted it because it sounded like a lesson.

Still true.

Koa came back.

"Can I eat the bread?"

Kalei said, "Yes."

He took the loaf.

Looked at the notebook.

"Any more crimes?"

Malia said, "Medication."

"Terrifying."

He left.

Keoni stood.

"I'm working."

Malia looked at him.

"That's it?"

"Yeah."

"We found notes that maybe show Tūtū saw objects move weird twenty-eight years ago."

"Maybe."

"And you're going to work."

"Definitely."

"What if it matters?"

Keoni looked at her.

"Then it'll still matter after I finish the outboard."

Malia hated how often that helped.

He left.

Nalani picked up the notebook.

Not possessively.

Different.

She turned pages slowly.

Malia watched her.

"What are you looking for?"

"I don't know."

"That's dangerous."

Nalani almost smiled.

Then stopped at a page.

Her finger stayed there.

Malia waited.

Nalani closed the notebook.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Malia stared.

Nalani stood.

"We're past nothing."

Nalani looked at her.

The phrase belonged to earlier chapters.

It sounded different now.

"I know."

"What was it?"

Nalani held the notebook against her chest.

"Not today."

Kalei went still.

Malia noticed.

That meant something.

Not supernatural.

Family.

Malia did not push.

That surprised everyone.

Especially her.

Nalani took the notebook to Tūtū Lehua's room.

Malia almost followed.

Did not.

Kalei watched her choose not to.

"Growth?"

Malia looked at her.

"Fuck off."

Kalei smiled.

Small.

Then looked toward the hall.

The smile disappeared.

Malia knew Chapter Nineteen had just moved closer even without knowing that was what it was.

She went to the front door.

The key hung straight.

Loop behind.

Normal.

She did not touch it.

The old urge was still there.

Check.

Tilt.

Leave wrong.

See who fixes.

Now it had a handwriting.

Left pair straight.

Right pair crossed.

See who fixes.

Malia stood with her hands in her pockets.

She thought about the hotel.

A room attendant noticing a moved amenity.

A supervisor checking a seal.

A maintenance worker leaving a valve position marked because another shift needed to know whether somebody had touched it.

Observation could protect people.

Observation could also become leverage.

The eye was not the sin.

The secret test was.

The assumption that knowing gave you the right to arrange somebody else.

That was closer.

Malia looked toward the hallway where Nalani had taken the notebook.

Then toward the yard.

Keoni working.

Koa eating bread.

Kalei washing the breakfast cups again because somebody had left coffee in one.

Current people.

Current uses.

She had spent most of her adult life being proud that she could walk into a room and know what had happened.

She still was.

She should be.

But Lehua had known too.

And when knowing was not enough, Lehua had sometimes changed the room to make people reveal themselves.

Malia had done the same thing with the key.

Once.

Once was enough to recognize the method.

She did not only have Tūtū Lehua's hands.

She had been practicing one of her tricks.
