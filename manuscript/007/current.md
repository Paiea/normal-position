# The Old Shelf

The key was straight in the morning.

Malia saw it before she finished crossing the main room.

Leather loop behind.

Iron bow flat against the wall.

Exactly normal.

She stopped with one hand on the back of a chair.

For half a second, satisfaction came first.

Then she hated herself for it.

Nalani was asleep in the rear room.

Kalei was already outside.

Keoni's truck had been gone when Malia woke.

Koa's door was open and his bed empty.

The house was quiet enough that the refrigerator clicking off sounded important.

Malia walked to the key.

Did not touch it.

Yesterday she had left it leaning right with the loop forward.

Now it had been corrected.

Observed fact.

That was all.

She heard sandals on the lanai.

Koa came in carrying a plastic bag of wet clothes.

Malia turned.

He stopped.

"What?"

"Did you use the front key last night?"

Koa stared at her.

"Morning."

"Did you?"

"Yeah."

"When?"

"After Micah's."

Malia's satisfaction died so fast it was almost embarrassing.

"You went out?"

"I told Dad."

"You didn't tell me."

Koa looked genuinely confused.

"Why would I?"

Fair.

"What time?"

"I don't know. Nine?"

"And you locked the door when you came back."

"Yeah."

"Then put the key back."

"Yeah."

Malia looked at the hook.

Koa followed her eyes.

His face changed.

Not much.

Enough.

"You did something to it."

"No."

"You did."

"I didn't."

"That's your lying voice."

"I do not have a lying voice."

"You have like six work voices."

He shifted the bag to his other hand.

"Was this the test?"

Malia looked at him.

Koa smiled.

"Oh, wow."

"It wasn't a test."

"What'd you do?"

"Nothing."

"Family word."

He walked past her toward the laundry area.

Malia followed him two steps.

"Did you notice anything when you took it?"

"The key?"

"Yes."

"No."

"Was the loop forward?"

Koa stopped again.

"I don't know."

"Was it leaning?"

"I don't know."

"You hung it straight."

"Because that's how it goes."

There it was.

No mystery.

No corrected object.

No impossible return.

A sixteen-year-old had come home, locked the door, and put the key away the way he had seen it put away for most of his life.

Malia folded her arms.

Koa watched her.

"You gonna tell me what you did?"

"No."

"Then I'm gonna assume you licked it."

"I hate you."

"No, you don't."

He went to deal with his wet clothes.

Malia stood beside the key.

Her test had worked perfectly.

It had tested whether somebody in the house knew the normal position.

A question whose answer had been obvious before she asked it.

She went to the kitchen and made coffee.

The rain had stopped before dawn.

Water still ticked from the gutters.

The yard smelled like wet grass, damp wood, and the faint metal smell that came after a hard night of rain.

Kalei was at the catchment tank with a flashlight.

Malia carried her coffee out.

"Problem?"

"Gauge is sticking."

"Bad?"

"No."

"Everyone says no before giving me the problem."

"Because you ask like something's on fire."

Kalei tapped the gauge housing.

The needle moved.

"See."

Malia saw.

"Do we need to do anything?"

"We need to not hit it harder."

"Strong maintenance plan."

Kalei gave her the flashlight.

"Hold."

Malia held it while Kalei opened the small access cover.

The work required two hands and no conversation.

That helped.

After a minute Kalei said, "Koa said you were testing the key."

Malia nearly moved the flashlight.

"He talks too much."

"He is sixteen."

"Apparently that's a diagnosis."

"It explains plenty."

Malia aimed the light lower.

"It wasn't a test."

Kalei looked at her.

Malia corrected herself.

"Fine. It was a tiny test."

"Of what?"

"I wanted to see if anybody corrected it."

"Why?"

Malia hated that the question sounded so simple.

"Because it keeps ending up exactly where it belongs."

"It's a key."

"I know."

Kalei wiped rainwater from the housing.

"You think somebody is moving it?"

"Somebody is moving it."

"Yes. We use it."

"You know what I mean."

Kalei closed the access cover.

"No. I know what you want me to mean."

Malia handed back the flashlight.

Kalei took it.

"Don't test people without telling them."

"I wasn't testing people."

"You changed something to see who would change it back."

Malia opened her mouth.

Kalei waited.

Malia closed it.

"That's people," Kalei said.

Then she walked toward the cookhouse.

Malia stayed beside the catchment tank.

There were moments when being corrected felt useful.

This was not one of them.

By eight, the cookhouse was busy enough to make irritation impractical.

Aunty Nani arrived with rice and left with an empty container.

Nalani made coffee and abandoned the brown cup beside the sink.

Keoni came through looking for a dry rag, drank water from one of the metal cups, and disappeared toward the workshop.

Koa ate standing up because he had somewhere to be and refused to specify where beyond "school thing."

"On Sunday?" Malia asked.

Koa looked at her.

"Do you know what day it is?"

Malia paused.

He smiled.

"Exactly."

She threw a dish towel at him.

He caught it, dried his hands with it, and left it on the counter.

"Put that back."

He came through the doorway again, took the towel, hung it on the peg, and left without speaking.

Kalei watched from the stove.

"You're making progress."

"With him?"

"With becoming Lehua."

Malia stared.

Kalei stirred the pot.

"Too soon?"

"Yes."

"Okay."

Malia did not know whether to be angry.

That was probably why Kalei had said it.

The morning broke apart after that.

Nalani wanted to go through paperwork from the filing cabinet.

Keoni needed someone to hold a length of tubing while he checked a leak near the workshop sink.

Aunty Nani came back because the container she had taken was not hers.

Then it turned out the lid was hers.

Nobody was surprised except Malia.

By ten, she had stopped monitoring the key.

On purpose.

She helped Nalani sort utility bills from old medical statements.

She found three receipts for repairs Keoni said had never actually been completed.

She carried a stack of towels from Tūtū Lehua's room to the laundry.

She put Kalei's blue sewing kit on the main table so Kalei would remember to take it back.

Ordinary work.

Useful work.

No experiments.

At eleven, she went to the cookhouse for water.

She stopped in the doorway.

The shelves were wrong.

Not one cup.

All of them.

Malia did not move.

The current system had disappeared.

Not completely.

Enough.

The white visitor cups were no longer lined across the top shelf.

Four sat together on the lower shelf.

Two remained high, shoved toward the center.

The everyday cups had spread upward.

One pale chipped cup sat at the far left of the top shelf.

The blue cup sat at the far right.

On the middle shelf:

brown cup left.

Another pale cup near center.

Green cracked cup right.

Metal cups had been pushed low.

Malia stared.

Her first thought was stupid.

Nohea.

Not because the blue cup belonged to Nohea.

It did not.

Because the positions did.

Left high.

Right high.

Adults through the middle.

Work cups low.

She knew the shape.

Not perfectly.

Enough.

The shelf had become a room in memory.

Malia crossed the threshold.

She did not touch anything.

The green cup's crack faced outward.

Even wrong in time, somebody had respected the current safety rule.

That made it worse.

Or better.

She could not tell.

Kalei came in behind her carrying a bag of onions.

Malia raised one hand.

"Don't."

Kalei stopped.

"What?"

"Look at the shelves."

Kalei looked.

She did not react immediately.

Then her eyes moved left to right.

Top.

Middle.

Bottom.

Her mouth tightened.

Malia watched her aunt recognize it.

"You see it."

Kalei put the onions on the table.

"Who did this?"

"I don't know."

"Did you?"

"No."

Kalei looked toward the yard.

"Koa?"

"Gone."

"Keoni?"

"Workshop."

"Nalani?"

"Paperwork."

Kalei stepped closer.

Malia said, "Don't move anything."

Kalei looked at her.

Malia heard herself.

There it was again.

Her test voice.

Her control voice.

She lowered her hand.

"Sorry. I just want to know if I'm remembering it right."

Kalei looked back at the shelves.

"You mostly are."

"Mostly?"

"The metal cups weren't all low."

"Where?"

"Two low. One middle."

"Whose?"

"Your grandfather's."

Malia blinked.

She had forgotten there had been a specific cup for him.

Not forgotten him.

Just the cup.

Kalei pointed without touching.

"Kids high. You left. Nohea right."

Malia swallowed.

"Adults middle."

"Mostly."

"Mom left."

Kalei nodded.

"You center?"

"Later."

"Tūtū right?"

"Depends when."

Malia looked at the green cup.

Right side middle shelf.

"Was green hers?"

"That green one? Later, yeah."

"But not back then."

"No."

"So the objects are wrong."

Kalei looked at her.

"The objects are current."

"The positions are old."

"Some of them."

Malia stared at the shelf.

That was exactly it.

Current things.

Older grammar.

Nalani came in carrying a folder.

"Why are you both standing like somebody died?"

Neither answered.

Nalani looked at the shelves.

Her face changed faster than Kalei's had.

"Oh."

Malia turned.

"You remember it."

Nalani walked closer.

"Kind of."

"Kind of?"

"I remember the children high."

Malia pointed.

"Me left. Nohea right."

Nalani nodded.

"Yes."

"You middle-left."

"For a while."

"Kalei center."

"Sometimes."

"Tūtū right."

Nalani gave a dry laugh.

"Tūtū was wherever she wanted."

Kalei said, "That's not useful."

"It is historically accurate."

Malia looked from one sister to the other.

"Why was it arranged like that?"

Kalei said, "So everybody knew whose was whose."

Nalani said, "So Mom knew who drank what."

Kalei sighed.

"Here we go."

Malia held up a hand.

"No. Both can be true."

Nalani looked at her.

Malia pointed to the top shelf.

"Children high because we had to ask."

"Yes," Kalei said.

Nalani added, "Or drag a chair over when she wasn't looking."

Malia looked at her.

A memory opened.

Nohea barefoot on the seat of a kitchen chair.

One knee on the backrest.

Reaching.

Malia standing in the doorway saying, You're going to fall.

Nohea saying, Then catch me.

Not scared.

Annoyed.

She had wanted Malia's cup.

Not her own.

"She stole mine," Malia said.

Nalani smiled before she could stop herself.

"Constantly."

"Why?"

Kalei said, "You had the green flowers."

"No, hers had flowers."

"Later cup."

Malia frowned.

Nalani was smiling now.

"Nohea said water tasted colder from yours."

Malia remembered.

Nohea drinking from Malia's cup and returning it to the wrong side.

Malia reporting her.

Nohea denying it with water still on her upper lip.

Then bribing Malia the next day with half a li hing mui candy she had hidden in a pocket until it was mostly lint.

Malia laughed.

It came out wrong.

Not sad.

Too sudden.

She covered her mouth.

Nalani's smile stayed.

Kalei looked at the shelves.

"She was irritating."

"She was seven."

"Those are compatible."

Malia lowered her hand.

For a minute Nohea was not dead.

Not exactly.

She was a kid climbing furniture because another cup allegedly made water colder.

Then Keoni came through the doorway.

He looked at the three women.

"What?"

Malia pointed to the shelves.

He looked.

"Someone cleaned."

"Keoni."

"What?"

"Look at the arrangement."

He did.

Longer this time.

"Huh."

That was all.

Malia waited.

Keoni opened a drawer and took out a rag.

"You remember it?"

"Some."

"Does it look old?"

"Yeah."

"How old?"

He shrugged.

"Before the cookhouse got rebuilt."

Kalei said, "Parts of it."

Keoni nodded.

Malia stared.

"Why aren't you more bothered?"

He looked at her.

"Because five people used this kitchen this morning."

"Who moved what?"

"No idea."

"Did you put the metal cups low?"

"I put one low."

"Which?"

He pointed.

One metal cup at the bottom.

Human explanation.

Nalani said, "I moved the white cups."

Malia turned.

"What?"

"They were in my way when I was looking for the tea."

"You moved them to the bottom?"

"Three of them."

Kalei looked.

"Four are down there."

Nalani shrugged.

"I moved three."

Human explanation.

Kalei pointed at the green cup.

"I washed that after breakfast."

"Did you put it there?"

"Probably."

"Why middle right?"

"Because there was space."

Human explanation.

Malia looked at the pale chipped cup, top left.

Koa could have moved it.

Aunty Nani could have moved it.

Malia herself could have moved something earlier and forgotten because putting away cups no longer registered as an event.

Every object had a path.

Every position had a person nearby.

Nothing on the shelves required anything impossible.

Together, the arrangement still made the back of Malia's neck tighten.

Keoni took the rag.

"Are we done?"

"No."

"I'm done."

He left.

Nalani looked at Malia.

"Don't."

"I haven't done anything."

"Your face has."

Malia turned back to the shelf.

"Nobody arranged this."

Kalei said, "Somebody arranged every part of it."

"That's not what I mean."

"I know."

Nalani set the folder on the table.

"What do you want to do?"

Malia almost said leave it.

Photograph it.

Write down who touched what.

Ask everybody.

Reconstruct.

The list arrived automatically.

She hated that too.

Instead she took out the blue cup.

Kalei watched.

Malia put it on the lower shelf.

Current normal.

Then the pale cup.

Lower.

Brown.

Lower.

Green crack outward.

Lower.

White visitor cups high.

Metal middle.

One by one, she restored the present arrangement.

Nalani said, "You're sure?"

Malia looked at her.

"No."

That was new.

She moved the final white cup to the top shelf.

The kitchen looked ordinary again.

Useful.

Current.

Malia stood with one hand on the shelf edge.

The arrangement she had just erased remained perfectly visible in her head.

It had not been random enough.

It had not been impossible enough.

Kalei picked up the onions again.

"What are you calling it?"

Malia looked at her.

"What?"

"You have a face when you're naming something."

Nalani said, "She does."

Malia ignored both of them.

Wrong shelf was too small.

Wrong place was not right either.

Every cup had been in a place a cup could reasonably be.

Nothing had been misplaced in space.

The mistake was somewhere else.

Malia looked at the current shelves.

Then at the version still sitting behind them in memory.

"Wrong in time," she said.

Neither sister laughed.
