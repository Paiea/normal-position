# Nothing Happened

Nothing happened before breakfast.

Malia checked.

Not dramatically.

She went into Tūtū Lehua's room to put away a folded towel.

The chair was where it had been.

The floor beside it was empty.

The comb sat in the ceramic dish.

The top dresser drawer was closed now because Kalei had finished with the medication packs.

The room looked more finished than it had the day before.

Not preserved.

Used less.

Malia put the towel on the shelf.

Then she left.

Nothing happened while she made coffee.

Nothing happened while Koa complained that the rain had made one of his shoes smell bad.

Nothing happened while Nalani tried to determine whether a utility account was still in Tūtū Lehua's name.

Nothing happened while Kalei stood under the lanai and shook a wet towel hard enough to spray Malia's ankles.

"Seriously?"

"You were standing there."

"That is not consent."

Kalei hung the towel.

"Move faster."

Malia looked toward the main-house window.

Tūtū Lehua's room was behind it.

She hated herself a little.

Then Keoni came through the gate at a jog.

That was unusual enough to clear her head.

He crossed the yard, went straight to the workshop, and came back with a heavy tool bag.

Kalei looked over.

"What happened?"

"Winch stripped."

"Whose?"

"Kimo."

"Boat in?"

"Half."

Kalei closed her eyes.

Malia said, "Half where?"

"On the trailer."

"That sounds bad."

"It isn't good."

Keoni looked at her.

"You busy?"

Malia glanced toward the house.

Nalani was on the phone.

Koa was putting on the less-smelly shoe.

Kalei had laundry.

"No."

"Come."

Malia stared.

"Why?"

"I need another person."

"For what?"

"Hands."

"I have those."

"Good."

He handed her the tool bag.

It was heavier than expected.

Malia almost dropped it.

Keoni did not comment.

That made it worse.

She followed him to the truck.

Koa called after them, "Bring food."

Keoni shouted, "No."

Koa said, "Then don't come back."

Keoni opened the passenger door for Malia by pulling it twice because the handle stuck.

She put the tool bag on the floor.

"Why is your truck like this?"

"Like what?"

"Every part has a workaround."

"It runs."

"That's not the same as functioning."

"It is the important part."

He started the engine.

Malia looked at him.

Keoni looked back.

"What?"

"Nothing."

He pulled onto the road.

The drive took less than fifteen minutes.

Long enough for Malia to realize she had left her phone on the kitchen table.

Long enough for that to bother her.

Long enough for her to consider turning back.

She did not.

Keoni drove with one hand on the wheel and the other resting near the gear shift.

Malia looked out at wet roadside grass, low clouds caught against the slopes, houses with laundry pulled under cover, trucks with fishing poles angled from beds.

Normal world.

People doing morning things.

No one cared where Tūtū Lehua's slippers were.

Keoni said, "You gonna ask?"

Malia looked at him.

"Ask what?"

"Whatever you've been thinking since we left."

"I can think without asking."

"New skill."

She ignored that.

"What happens if the boat slides?"

"It won't."

"What if it does?"

"Then Kimo has a worse morning."

"That's not reassuring."

"You're not supposed to be reassured."

Malia looked at him.

Keoni smiled.

There was a version of him she remembered from being kids.

Same mouth.

Different face.

He had been the cousin who took apart toys he did not own and then got offended when people called them broken before he finished putting them back together.

"What does stripped mean?" she asked.

"The ratchet teeth are gone."

"All of them?"

"Enough."

"So it won't hold."

"It'll hold until it doesn't."

"Great."

"We chained the boat before I left."

"Who is we?"

"Kimo."

"Anybody else?"

"His brother."

"So why do you need me?"

"They're both under it mentally."

Malia laughed.

"What does that mean?"

"You'll see."

She did.

Kimo and his brother were standing beside the trailer arguing about whether the winch handle had always made that noise.

The boat sat crooked on the trailer, bow not fully seated.

A chain ran from the bow eye to the trailer frame.

The winch cable sagged.

Kimo saw Keoni's truck and raised both arms.

"Finally."

Keoni looked at the clock.

"It has been eighteen minutes."

"Long eighteen."

His brother pointed at the winch.

"I told him stop cranking."

Kimo said, "After it broke."

"I told you before."

"You said it sounded ugly."

"Same thing."

Keoni walked past both of them.

Malia carried the tool bag.

Kimo looked at her.

"Malia?"

She nodded.

He smiled.

"Wow."

She had no idea what that meant.

"Hi."

"You remember me?"

"Should I?"

Kimo laughed.

"Good. Same."

That helped.

Keoni crouched beside the winch.

"Nobody touch anything."

Kimo's brother lifted both hands.

Kimo said, "Wasn't."

Keoni looked at the handle.

Turned it slightly.

Stopped.

He pressed one thumb against the ratchet gear.

Then reached under the housing.

Malia watched.

"What do you need?"

"Bag."

She put it beside him.

"Open."

She opened it.

Inside was not order.

It was a crime scene.

Sockets loose in one compartment.

Two screwdrivers crossing each other.

Pliers under electrical tape.

Three bolts that apparently belonged nowhere.

A piece of folded sandpaper.

A small hammer.

A wrench with one end wrapped in blue tape.

Malia stared.

"Which?"

"Half-inch socket."

She looked.

There were six sockets.

"Which one's half?"

Keoni held out his hand without looking.

Malia picked one.

"Too small."

Another.

"Too big."

Kimo's brother said, "This is fun."

Malia glared.

Keoni reached into the bag without looking and pulled out the correct socket.

Malia stared at him.

"You knew where that was."

"Yeah."

"Then why didn't you get it?"

"I was holding this."

His other hand was stabilizing the winch housing.

Reasonable.

Annoying.

He loosened two bolts.

Kimo asked, "Can you fix it?"

"Maybe."

"That sounds bad."

"It's honest."

Keoni removed the cover.

Metal shavings sat inside.

He touched them.

Then the ratchet wheel.

One side of several teeth had rounded almost smooth.

"How long you been forcing it?"

Kimo said, "I haven't."

His brother laughed.

Keoni looked at Kimo.

Kimo looked away.

"Yesterday."

"How many times?"

"Some."

"Number."

"Four."

His brother said, "Eight."

Kimo turned.

"You counting now?"

"I have ears."

Keoni exhaled.

Malia recognized the expression.

Not anger.

Calculation with disappointment inside it.

"Why'd you keep using it?"

Kimo pointed at the boat.

"Because I needed the boat out."

"Then you needed a working winch."

"That's why I called you."

"After."

"Yes."

Keoni looked at Malia.

"See?"

"See what?"

"People."

She laughed.

He went back to work.

They could not fix the winch properly there.

That became clear after ten minutes.

The replacement ratchet Keoni had brought did not match the shaft.

Kimo wanted to grind it.

Keoni said no.

Kimo wanted to drill it.

Keoni said no.

Kimo's brother wanted breakfast.

Everyone agreed.

The actual solution was less satisfying.

They repositioned the boat using a come-along from Keoni's truck, kept the safety chain engaged, and transferred load slowly while Keoni checked each connection twice.

Malia's job was to hold tension on one line and say if the bow shifted sideways.

That was all.

It required attention.

Real attention.

Not interpretive attention.

If the boat moved left, she said left.

If the line slackened, she said slack.

If Keoni told her stop, she stopped.

No theories.

No memory.

No older normal.

Just weight.

She liked it.

For twenty minutes, nothing existed except the boat's position in the present.

Keoni said, "Two inches."

Malia watched the bow.

"One."

Kimo cranked.

"Half."

"Stop."

Everyone stopped.

Keoni checked the chain.

Then the line.

Then the trailer.

"Again."

They worked.

When the bow finally seated where it needed to, Kimo slapped the side of the boat.

Keoni said, "Don't."

Kimo stopped.

"Why?"

"Because I don't trust you near mechanical systems right now."

His brother laughed so hard he had to turn away.

Kimo pointed at Keoni.

"You gonna fix the winch?"

"At the shop."

"Today?"

"No."

"Why?"

"I have other work."

Kimo looked offended.

Malia understood that expression too.

Some people believed asking for help promoted their problem to first place.

Keoni's phone buzzed.

He checked it.

His expression changed.

Not much.

Malia had started noticing how little his face needed to move before the answer became no.

"What?"

"Nothing."

She looked at him.

He sighed.

"Guy with the outboard."

"What about him?"

"Was supposed to meet him at ten."

Malia checked the time.

Ten nineteen.

"Did you tell him?"

"Texted before we started."

"And?"

"He has work at noon."

"So you lose the job?"

"No. Probably tomorrow."

Probably.

There it was again.

A word that made everyone else in the family furious and seemed perfectly normal in Keoni's mouth.

Kimo heard enough to understand.

"Eh. My fault."

Keoni kept packing.

"You called."

"I didn't know you had work."

"You called because you needed help."

"Still."

Kimo went to the back of his truck and opened a cooler.

He came back with a plastic bag wrapped around two frozen packs of fish.

Keoni looked at it.

"No."

"For Kalei."

"No."

"Kalei likes akule."

"Everybody likes akule."

"Then take."

Keoni stared at him.

Kimo held the bag farther out.

"You miss your job because my stupid winch, I give fish."

"That's not how invoices work."

"Good thing I'm not paying an invoice."

Kimo's brother said, "Take the fish before he starts talking about friendship."

Kimo turned.

"Fuck you."

Keoni took the bag.

"Mahalo."

Kimo nodded once.

Done.

Malia watched the whole exchange.

No ledger.

No balance achieved.

Keoni had lost part of a workday.

Kimo had given fish.

The fish did not equal the lost job.

Nobody pretended it did.

Still, the exchange mattered.

Keoni packed the failed ratchet into his bag.

Kimo said, "Tomorrow?"

"Maybe."

Malia smiled.

Keoni looked at her.

She looked away.

On the drive back, Keoni stopped at a small store without explanation.

"Food?" Malia asked.

"Filter."

"For the catchment?"

"No."

She waited.

Keoni went inside.

He came back with two sandwiches, a bottle of water, and no filter.

Malia looked at the bag.

"Strong repair plan."

"They didn't have it."

"You bought sandwiches."

"I adapted."

He handed her one.

Malia took it.

"Mahalo."

They ate in the truck with the windows cracked.

Rain started again.

Light.

Keoni unwrapped half his sandwich.

Malia said, "Do people call you every time something breaks?"

"No."

"Often?"

"Yeah."

"Why?"

He chewed.

"Because I can fix things."

"That sounds exhausting."

"Sometimes."

"You can say no."

"I do."

Malia waited.

Keoni looked at her.

"What?"

"That sounded like there was a second half."

"There isn't."

"You're bad at this."

"At what?"

"Talking."

"I'm eating."

She looked through the windshield.

A truck passed.

Water hissed under tires.

Keoni said, "You want me to say kuleana?"

Malia turned.

He was still eating.

She almost laughed.

"I wasn't."

"You were getting there."

"I was not."

"You get a face."

"Everybody needs to stop saying that."

Keoni swallowed.

"People help me too."

Malia waited.

"That's it?"

"Yeah."

"That's your whole philosophy."

"Pretty much."

He folded the sandwich wrapper.

"Kimo helps with fish when he has extra. Nani watches Koa if I need. Kalei did everything when Dad was sick. I fix a winch."

"That's not equal."

"No."

"Doesn't bother you?"

"Sometimes."

Malia looked at him.

Keoni shrugged.

"If I wait until everything is equal, nobody gets anything done."

That stayed with her.

Not because it solved the family place.

It did not.

Nalani was still allowed to ask who had paid what.

Kalei was still allowed to resent being the person who stayed.

Malia was still allowed to leave.

Nothing about reciprocal work erased imbalance.

Still.

Keoni did not seem interested in turning every obligation into proof of love or exploitation.

Sometimes a winch was broken.

Somebody knew how to fix it.

That was enough for the morning.

When they got back, Koa was sitting under the lanai with three other teenagers and a pile of poster board.

Malia looked at him.

"School thing."

Koa did not look up.

"Told you."

One of the girls was drawing lettering with a ruler.

Another was eating chips.

The boy beside Koa was cutting photos.

Malia said, "What is it?"

"History presentation."

"On?"

Koa looked at her.

"History."

She stared.

He smiled.

Keoni walked past them.

"Don't ask."

Malia carried the tool bag to the workshop.

She set it down where Keoni pointed.

Not where she thought it belonged.

That mattered.

Then she went inside.

Nalani was on the phone.

Kalei was folding laundry.

The front key was on its hook.

Straight.

Loop behind.

Malia saw it.

Kept walking.

She went to Tūtū Lehua's room.

The floor beside the chair remained empty.

The comb sat in its dish.

Nothing under the bed.

Nothing behind the chair.

Nothing strange in the drawer.

Malia stood there.

She had spent four hours away.

Plenty of time.

If something wanted to happen when she was not looking, it had been given excellent service.

Nothing had.

Kalei appeared behind her with folded sheets.

"You checking?"

Malia looked at the chair.

"Yeah."

Kalei stepped into the room.

"And?"

"Nothing."

"Good."

Malia turned.

"Good?"

Kalei put the sheets on the shelf.

"Would you prefer something?"

Malia did not answer.

Kalei looked at her.

Malia looked away first.

That was answer enough.

She left the room.

The rest of the afternoon refused to become meaningful.

Koa and his friends finished the poster.

Nalani found the missing utility login in Tūtū Lehua's old email notes.

Keoni discovered the replacement ratchet he needed was in a parts bin under a completely different label because he had salvaged it two years ago from another winch.

Kalei burned the first batch of rice because Aunty Nani called.

They ate the second batch.

Malia washed dishes.

The cups stayed in current order.

She checked once.

Then, embarrassed, checked again.

Still current.

The front key moved twice.

Both times she saw who used it.

Once Nalani.

Once Koa.

The comb stayed in its dish.

The slippers stayed gone.

No older arrangement emerged.

No route cleared itself through accident.

No one remembered anything they had not meant to remember.

Nothing happened.

At sunset, Malia sat on the lanai with one of Keoni's unopened sodas.

He came out carrying a small metal part wrapped in a rag.

"That's the ratchet?"

"Yeah."

"Thought you didn't have one."

"Forgot I did."

She looked at him.

"That feels relevant."

"It isn't."

"Probably not."

He sat on the step below her.

For a while they listened to rain collect in the gutter.

Malia said, "I think I wanted something to happen today."

Keoni turned the part in his hand.

"Why?"

"So I could stop wondering."

"Wouldn't stop."

"You don't know that."

He looked at her.

She sighed.

"Fine."

Keoni wiped one edge of the ratchet with the rag.

"Something weird can be weird without becoming your job."

Malia stared at him.

He kept working.

There it was.

Not mystical.

Not profound.

Annoyingly useful.

She drank the soda.

Behind them, the house stayed exactly where it was.

So did everything inside it.
