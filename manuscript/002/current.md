# Keeping Days

By eight in the morning, the house had already borrowed four chairs, lent out two pans, misplaced a serving knife, and acquired three people Mara had never met.

The front key was on its hook.

Mara checked before she meant to.

Straight against the stone. Leather loop behind.

She looked away before the key could become a problem.

Outside, the compound had the soft, temporary disorder of a place expecting more bodies than it normally held.

Mrs. Aren's two folding chairs were open under the main-house window. Someone had set a low stool beside the cistern. Two baskets of bread waited just inside the kitchen door with strips of cloth tied around the handles so they could be returned to the right households later.

Lio was carrying a stack of bowls across the yard.

"Those are wet," Mara said.

"They're dry."

"They're shining."

"That's glaze."

She stepped closer.

He tilted the stack toward her.

Dry.

Mara backed off.

Lio did not say anything.

That was worse.

"Enjoy this," she told him. "It won't happen often."

"I'm going to remember it forever."

"Then you'll finally have something useful to do with your adolescence."

He carried the bowls into the kitchen.

Inez had already started another pot.

Of course she had.

There were six different foods on the worktable, only two of which Mara believed had originated in the Orlan kitchen. Keeping Days produced meals the way storms produced driftwood. Nobody was quite sure where everything had started, only that eventually somebody had to sort what belonged where.

Inez pointed with a spoon.

"Lower shelf."

Mara followed the direction.

The cup shelves ran along the eastern wall above the washing basin. Three open wooden shelves, all darkened at the front edge from years of hands.

The lower shelf held the everyday cups.

Brown glaze. Blue glaze. Two pale ones with chips near the handles. A green cup with a hairline crack that made Mara immediately dislike it.

The middle shelf held larger drinking bowls and the metal cups people took into the yard.

The top shelf held six matched white cups with thin painted lines near the rim.

Visitors.

Mara put the bowls down instead.

"Lower shelf," Inez repeated.

"Those are bowls."

"The cups behind you."

Mara turned.

Lio had placed eight clean cups on the table behind her.

He had vanished.

Coward.

She picked them up.

"All of these low?"

"Everyday cups low. White ones high."

Mara slid the brown, blue, green, and pale cups onto the lower shelf.

"The brown one too?"

Inez looked over.

"Why wouldn't it?"

"Mother used it yesterday."

"Your mother uses whatever she reaches first."

True.

Mara put the brown cup beside the blue one.

Inez went back to the pot.

The lower shelf was crowded, but not badly. Handles mostly turned outward. Nothing stacked. Enough room between cups that taking one did not knock the others.

Mara adjusted the green cup so the crack faced the wall.

"Don't hide it," Inez said.

Mara stopped.

"What?"

"The crack. Turn it out."

"Why?"

"So nobody gives it to a visitor."

Mara turned the crack toward the room.

Practical.

She approved.

The top shelf, she noticed, had once been the everyday shelf.

She remembered reaching up for cups as a child and having to use both hands.

Or maybe she remembered Adela telling her not to reach.

The memory was too small to bother verifying.

Behind her, Inez lifted the lid from the pot.

Steam rolled toward the open window.

"Take the bread to the main house before Celina starts putting paper on the food table."

Mara took both baskets.

"She's already putting paper on the food table."

Inez closed her eyes briefly.

"Then move the paper."

"She'll kill me."

"She's your mother."

"Exactly."

The main house had become an administrative office against its will.

Celina had claimed one end of the long table with estate documents, an ink pen, two pencils, a ruler, and three piles that Mara assumed meant something.

At the other end, a man Mara did not know was eating bread over a plate while explaining roof sealant to an older woman.

Between them sat a bowl of stewed fruit.

Mara put the bread beside it.

Celina immediately moved the nearest basket six inches away from her papers.

"Thank you."

"You are on the food table."

"I'm on one corner."

"You're on the wrong corner."

"There are no assigned corners."

Mara looked toward the east room.

Adela's door remained closed.

"No?"

Celina followed her eyes and exhaled through her nose.

"We can start with the main room."

"It's Keeping Days."

"We are not distributing anything."

"I know."

"So counting a chair does not offend the dead."

From the doorway, the man eating bread said, "Depends on the chair."

Celina turned.

He raised both hands.

"I've been married thirty-eight years. I know when to stop."

The older woman beside him slapped his forearm.

Mara bit the inside of her cheek.

Celina stared at them for a second, then started laughing.

The tension cracked just enough to let the room breathe again.

"Fine," Celina said. "No chairs."

Inez came in carrying a covered dish.

"No inventory today."

Celina's face changed immediately.

"I said no distribution."

"I heard you."

"Then what's the problem?"

"The problem is six more people are coming before noon, two are staying through supper, and Tomas said Paren's sister is sleeping here tonight because the guest room at Aren's is full."

Celina looked at Mara.

"Do you see what she does?"

Inez set the dish down.

"I'm standing in front of you."

"What you do is make logistics sound like law."

"What I do is know where people are sleeping."

"That's because you volunteered the house."

"It's Keeping Days."

"Yes. Which apparently means nobody can count a spoon."

"You can count spoons while you wash them."

Celina sat back.

Mara took one step toward the kitchen.

Both women looked at her.

"No," Mara said.

"I didn't ask anything," Celina said.

"Neither did I," Inez said.

"Your faces did."

The man with the bread stood up.

"I suddenly remember somewhere else I'm meant to be."

His wife caught his sleeve.

"You sit."

He sat.

Mara took the empty stewed-fruit bowl and left.

The yard was better.

The yard had wind.

Tomas came through the west opening carrying a metal housing under one arm.

He looked as though he had slept badly and considered that everybody else's problem.

"Generator worked," Mara said.

"For eleven hours."

"That's not a success?"

"It needed twelve."

"How specific."

"Cold box warmed before they got back."

He set the housing on the ground outside the gear house.

"What is that?"

"Pump cover."

"Should it be off?"

"No."

She looked at him.

He looked at her.

"Different machine."

"Good."

"No, actually."

He crouched beside the cover and ran his thumb along a split near one mounting hole.

Mara watched him inspect it.

No dramatics. No sighing. Thumb over metal. Turn. Press. Check again.

"Can it be fixed?"

"Yes."

"Today?"

"That's the irritating part."

He stood.

"You still have the west key?"

Tomas touched his pocket.

Then frowned.

He checked the other pocket.

Mara's attention sharpened before she gave it permission.

Tomas went to the main house door.

The small brass key hung on the lower hook beneath the front key.

He took it down.

"Did you put that back last night?"

"Must have."

"You didn't come back before we closed."

"I came through the west after."

"Inez barred it."

"She unbarred it."

Mara looked toward the kitchen.

"Okay."

Tomas paused.

"Was there going to be a second part?"

"No."

"Good talk."

He went back toward the gear house with the brass key.

Mara watched him go.

Then she looked at the two hooks.

Main key high.

West key low.

Different metals. Different sizes. Different jobs.

She had known that yesterday.

Today it felt more settled.

A normal position was easiest to see after somebody used it correctly.

By ten, the compound filled.

People brought boiled roots, fish wrapped in leaves, fruit, tea, a pot of something Mara could not identify without opening it, and one enormous dish Inez immediately sent back because there was nowhere cool enough to keep it safely.

Nobody seemed offended.

The woman who had brought it said, "I'll return at four."

Inez said, "Five."

"Four thirty."

"Fine."

Mara watched the negotiation happen over food as if both women were settling dock rights.

Hospitality was not softness.

It was scheduling.

In the kitchen, Mara discovered a second system.

Beside the door stood a narrow return rack with three shelves.

Empty household containers went there after washing.

Top shelf for covered tins.

Middle for baskets.

Bottom for pots and heavy dishes.

Each item kept whatever string, cloth strip, painted mark, or scratched initial identified the owner.

Mara approved so strongly it was embarrassing.

She had spent years at the hotel trying to make departments stop leaving unclaimed containers in corridors.

Here, Inez had solved the problem with one shelf and social shame.

"That basket belongs to the north lane," Inez said.

Mara moved it from the middle shelf to the floor beside another.

"I thought baskets were middle."

"Those go back before supper."

"So the shelf is by type except when it's by time?"

"Yes."

"That's terrible."

"It works."

Lio, rinsing a pot, said, "You said that about the chairs."

"Because all of you are comfortable with chaos."

Inez pointed at the rack.

"Can you tell me whose containers are whose?"

Mara looked.

Paint mark.

Cloth strip.

Burn pattern.

One tin with a dent near the lid.

"Not yet."

"I can."

"Because you've known them forever."

"Exactly."

Mara had no answer to that.

The hotel had labels.

The village had memory.

Both worked until the person who remembered left.

She did not like where that thought went, so she picked up a towel.

By noon, three different people had told Mara stories about Adela.

None of them were about death.

One was about Adela refusing to pay for a badly repaired roof.

One was about her walking half a mile to return six coins a shopkeeper had given her by mistake.

One was about her accusing a neighbor's goat of learning to open a latch.

"Did the goat?"

Mara asked.

The old man telling the story chewed carefully.

"Eventually."

"That's not an answer."

"It became true."

He reached for another piece of bread.

Mara looked at Inez.

Inez was smiling into a pot.

That was worse than an answer too.

Around one, Celina tried inventory again.

She brought her papers into the kitchen.

Inez looked at them.

Celina put them on the far end of the worktable.

Inez said nothing.

Celina smiled with the satisfaction of someone who had won a legal case nobody else knew was happening.

Mara kept cutting fruit.

"Main room only," Celina said.

"Mm," Inez said.

"That means yes?"

"It means I heard you."

Celina looked at Mara.

Mara cut another piece of fruit.

"I am not translating."

"You're excellent at systems."

"People aren't systems."

Inez made a small sound.

Mara looked up.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Again.

Mara put the knife down.

"That is not going to become a family tradition."

"It already is," Celina said.

Lio came through the door carrying three folded blankets.

"Where?"

Inez pointed with her chin toward the main house.

"North room, top chest."

Mara looked at him.

"My north room?"

"The other north room."

"There are two?"

Lio stopped.

Mara stared back.

He shifted the blankets.

"You slept here as a kid."

"Twelve years ago."

"Still had two rooms."

"I thought one was storage."

"It was. Then it wasn't."

"Helpful."

He left.

Celina tapped the pencil against her papers.

"Blue chest. Rear west room. One."

Inez said, "You are not inventorying while pretending not to inventory."

"I said main room."

"The blue chest is not in the main room."

"Then I withdraw the chest."

"You already wrote it."

Celina crossed out the line with enough pressure to nearly tear the page.

Mara picked up the fruit knife again.

The dead did not need to haunt a house when their daughters could do this unassisted.

She did not say that.

Mostly because Inez would hear her.

The afternoon settled into a rhythm.

Visitors came in through the front.

The key stayed on its hook because the main door stayed open.

Food moved through the yard.

Dirty cups collected at the basin.

Clean cups returned to the lower shelf.

Everyday low.

White visitor cups high.

Metal cups middle.

Mara learned the pattern without deciding to.

She also learned that the green cracked cup belonged to nobody because everyone hated it but nobody would throw it away.

"Why keep it?" she asked.

Inez was scrubbing a pot.

"It's still a cup."

"It leaks?"

"No."

"Then fair."

"Also your grandmother liked it."

There it was.

Small enough to fit in one sentence.

Mara picked up the green cup.

Crack outward.

She put it back on the lower shelf.

By late afternoon, Celina had managed to inventory twelve items.

Six chairs, one long table, one writing desk, two lamps, one wall clock, and a ceramic bowl that Inez informed her belonged to Mrs. Aren.

Celina crossed out the bowl.

"See?" Mara said.

"I am building a record."

"You are building evidence that we borrow everything."

"Not everything."

Mara looked around.

Mrs. Aren's chairs.

Three borrowed pots.

Two baskets waiting for return.

A serving spoon with somebody else's initials scratched into the handle.

Celina followed her gaze.

"Fine. We borrow structurally."

That made Mara laugh.

Inez did not.

She was watching the clock.

"Tomas should be back."

"From?"

"South quay."

"What broke?"

"Tiller bracket."

"Same boat?"

"No."

Mara looked toward the gear house.

Empty.

"Tomas does this every day?"

"Things break every day."

Inez picked up the main-house lamp.

Not the one from the table. A squat metal lamp Mara had seen on the shelf by the front wall.

She checked the fuel by weight.

"Low."

Lio looked over.

"I filled it yesterday."

"Half."

"That counts as filled."

"It counts as half."

He rolled his eyes and took the lamp.

"Oil's in the gear house."

"Top left."

"I know where oil is."

"Then why are you still here?"

He left.

Mara smiled.

Inez saw.

"What?"

"Nothing."

Inez narrowed her eyes.

"That is mine."

"I learned from family."

At supper, only seven people remained.

That felt quiet.

Paren's sister was one of them, a woman named Tessa who had brought no luggage because somebody from Salen was apparently delivering it later. She ate with both elbows on the table and apologized to nobody.

Celina liked her immediately.

They spent half the meal discussing ferry schedules.

Mara sat beside Lio.

He had repaired the sandal again.

This time with wire.

"That's worse," Mara said.

"It's stronger."

"It's touching your foot."

"I have skin."

"Temporarily."

He ate another bite.

Mara noticed the cup in front of him.

Blue.

Lower shelf.

Celina had the brown one again.

Inez used one of the pale chipped cups.

Tessa had been given a white cup from the top shelf.

There it was.

The system working without anyone announcing it.

After supper, Mara washed.

Lio dried.

Inez put things away.

Celina had escaped to the main house with her papers.

Tessa had gone to help make up the spare bed because, in her words, she preferred knowing exactly what she was about to sleep on.

Mara liked her too.

The cups went home one by one.

Green crack outward.

Brown beside blue.

Pale cups to the left.

White cups high.

Metal cups middle.

Mara finished the last one and handed it to Lio.

He dried it.

White.

He reached for the lower shelf.

"High," Mara said.

He looked at her.

She looked at him.

He smiled.

"Been here one day."

"Still high."

He put it on the top shelf.

"You're getting worse."

"Professionally."

They finished the kitchen.

Inez checked the food storage.

Lio checked the cistern.

Mara carried two folded towels to the main house and found Celina asleep at the table with one hand resting on the inventory sheet.

Mara slid the pencil away before it rolled off.

She did not straighten the papers.

Progress.

At the front door, the key hung exactly where it should.

Mara touched the leather loop once.

Not to fix it.

Just to confirm it was flat.

That counted as progress too, depending on how generous a person felt.

She went to the north room.

The water cup was no longer on the sill.

Good.

The window had been shut.

Also good.

Her travel bag was gone.

Mara stopped.

The space beside the wall was empty.

For one second, the room rearranged itself in her head.

Bag beside wall.

Bed.

Blanket.

Cup.

Bag beside wall.

She crouched.

The bag had been pushed under the bed.

Still zipped.

Still closed.

Nothing missing that she could see.

Mara stayed crouched.

From the next room came Tessa's voice.

"Your aunt said I could put it there."

Mara looked toward the wall.

Tessa added, "We needed the floor for the bedding."

There was a pause.

"Was that wrong?"

Mara put one hand on the bag handle.

"No."

She pulled it halfway out, then stopped.

The room was small.

There really was nowhere better.

She pushed the bag back under the bed.

"No," Mara said again. "It's fine."

Tessa said, "All right."

A mattress rope creaked next door.

Mara stood.

Her bag remained under the bed.

Not where she had left it.

Exactly where somebody living had needed it to be.
